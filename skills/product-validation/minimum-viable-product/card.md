# Minimum Viable Product

## Essence

Minimum Viable Product (MVP) is not a stripped-down product but the cheapest means of testing the main assumption about the product on real people. Eric Ries defines an MVP as a version of a new product that allows the team to collect the maximum of validated learning about customers with the minimum of effort. The outcome is a decision.

## When to use and when not to

Use when it is unknown whether the product, or its key part, is needed: there is a hypothesis about the problem and value, but building a full solution is expensive.

Inputs: a formulated hypothesis, a riskiest assumption on which everything else depends, a group of real users, and a way to measure their behaviour.

Do not use:

- when the assumption is already confirmed and the product needs to be scaled;
- when an error is critical for safety or law (for example, payment operations without a licence): a "manual" MVP does not cancel these requirements;
- as a justification for a raw product: the goal of an MVP is learning, not releasing an unfinished product.

## Scheme

```
Hypothesis → the riskiest assumption
   ↓
Form of the MVP: what is real for the user / what is done by hand / what is not built
   ↓
Metric and success threshold (before launch) · sample · duration · budget
   ↓
Launch → Measurement → Conclusion   (build–measure–learn)
   ↓
Decision: continue · change course · stop
```

## How to apply

1. **Formulate the hypothesis and find the riskiest assumption.** For example: "people will buy shoes online without trying them on".
2. **Choose the form of the MVP according to the assumption.** You need a minimal version where the user performs a real action (purchase, payment, sign-up), while everything else can be replaced by manual work. Forms: one real scenario instead of the whole product; manual fulfilment behind a ready-made interface; manual service of individual customers without automation.
3. **Set the metric and the threshold before launch.** What, and in what number, counts as confirmation. Without a threshold, the result is interpreted however one likes.
4. **Define the sample, the duration and the budget.** These are real users, not acquaintances; the duration is weeks, not quarters.
5. **Launch, measure, and record the result** with facts: numbers, observations, feedback.
6. **Make the decision:** continue, change the hypothesis (pivot — a turn of strategy while keeping the goal), or stop.

## Example

Zappos (Nick Swinmurn), late 1990s. The year of the test is not given in the source: Fortune dates the launch of Shoesite.com (later Zappos) to 1999, after $150,000 had been raised. Swinmurn wanted to check that people buy shoes online. By his account, he went to the Footwear Etc. store in Sunnyvale (California) and proposed: "I'll take some pictures, put your shoes online, and if people buy them, I'll buy them from you at full price." The store agreed, and Swinmurn got "a few orders".

Reconstruction from the source: what was real for the buyer was the order placed online; the purchase of a pair from the store was done manually.

Decision per Fortune: after "a few orders", Swinmurn decided to put the whole collection online (after a shoe trade show) and raised $150,000 from friends, colleagues and other acquaintances.

The card author's conclusion: there was no threshold, so the decision was made without a formal criterion.

What the source does not include: a success metric and threshold, the number of orders and revenue, the duration of the test. The claim that he lost money on every sale is not confirmed.

Case source: [Fortune, «Nick Swinmurn: Zappos’ silent founder»](https://fortune.com/2012/09/05/nick-swinmurn-zappos-silent-founder/), 05.09.2012.

## Common mistakes

- **MVP as a "first version with minimal features".** The goal is lost: to test the assumption. Frank Robinson also defined an MVP as a "product of the right size", not as a stripped-down one.
- **No success threshold.** The result is explained after the fact.
- **Testing the wrong assumption.** Something beautiful is built, while the risk lies elsewhere: willingness to pay.
- **A survey instead of behaviour.** Words do not replace a purchase.
- **A sample of acquaintances.** They will give a polite "yes".
- **Too much that is real.** Eric Ries: IMVU's first MVP shipped in half a year (at the previous company the launch took almost five years), but a two-week feature that nobody wanted was "way too long": the lack of demand could have been discovered much earlier.
- **No decision.** The experiment is over, but nobody said "continue, change or stop".
- **Ignoring failure.** A weak result is read as "not enough features".
