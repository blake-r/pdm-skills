# Validation Board

## Essence

The Validation Board is a board for a team that does not yet know whether its idea has a customer and a problem. It was released by Lean Startup Machine in October 2012; according to the authors, the board was compiled after studying the work of more than 500 startups.

The board consists of two parts:

- **Hypothesis pivots** at the top: three rows — customer, problem, solution — and five columns: the start and four pivots. Each pivot is a new "customer — problem" pair, when the old one was not confirmed.
- **Experiment** at the bottom: the assumptions that must be true, the riskiest of them, the method of testing and the minimum success criterion. Next comes "get out of the building", and the sticky note with the assumption moves to the "invalidated" or "validated" column.

Working rules (the card author's reading of the board, not quoted from the authors): the assumption must be refutable, without subjective words. If the result reaches the criterion, the hypothesis is confirmed and you carry on. Refutation is a reason to pivot. The solution hypothesis is written only after the customer and the problem are confirmed.

## When to use and when not to

Use at the start of a new product or direction, when the team is confident in the idea but has not checked whether customers have such a problem. When the team keeps switching segments and wants to see what it has already rejected and why. When an idea needs to be tested cheaply before development.

Do not use for improvements to a working product where the customer and the problem are already known. Not suitable for precise measurement: the board records "yes/no" decisions against a threshold, not the size of an effect.

Limitations:

- **One assumption at a time.** The riskiest is tested first, the others wait.
- **Methods by increasing cost.** The board names three: Exploration (conversations with customers), Pitch (an offer to buy or sign up), Concierge (manual delivery of the service). An expensive method — only when a cheap one has already given confirmation.
- **A criterion does not save you from a bad sample.** Five answers out of ten from friends mean nothing; only customers are counted.

## Scheme

```
HYPOTHESIS PIVOTS   start │ pivot 1 │ 2 │ 3 │ 4
  customer            ... │   ...   │   │   │
  problem             ... │   ...   │   │   │
  solution                │         │   │   │
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
EXPERIMENT
  assumptions │ riskiest      │ → GET OUT  │ invalidated │
              │ method        │   OF THE   │ validated   │
              │ minimum success │ BUILDING │             │
```

## How to apply

1. **Write down the customer and the problem:** as specifically as possible, otherwise there will be no progress.
2. **List the assumptions** that must be true for the hypothesis to work.
3. **Choose the riskiest one** and phrase it so that it can be refuted.
4. **Choose the method and the criterion:** the cheapest method that will answer the question, and a number set before going out to customers.
5. **Go out to customers** and compare the result with the criterion.
6. **If refuted — pivot:** write the new "customer — problem" pair in the next column and repeat. **If confirmed** — take the next risky assumption, and move to the solution only after the customer and the problem.

## Example

Fashion Metric — a startup looking for whom and how to help when buying clothes. In 2012 Lean Startup Machine published its board step by step:

| Step | Customer and problem | Assumption | Method and criterion | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Start | shoppers who go for clothes alone; cannot decide what to buy | they need advice in real time | Exploration (conversations), 5/10 | invalidated |
| Pivot 1 | fashionable men aged 20–40 from big cities; hard to find clothes that fit well | they need help finding such clothes | Exploration (conversations), 5/10 | validated |
| Next | the same | will sign up for a service that helps find them | Pitch (offer to sign up), 80% sign up | validated |

Decision: change the customer and the problem — from help with choice to finding clothes by size and fit — and check sign-ups for the service.

In the board the "validated" notes are "People want help finding clothes that FIT" and "Will signup for service to help find clothes that FIT" (the case page shows only images; read from them). What the case does not include: how many people were surveyed and how many agreed — only the thresholds; the "solution" row is empty; the company does not provide dates or further results. The case was published by the board's authors as a teaching example.

Case source: Lean Startup Machine, [Validation Board page](https://web.archive.org/web/20121030161806/http://leanstartupmachine.com/validationboard/), section "Case Study 2", archive snapshot of 30.10.2012.

## Common mistakes

- **The customer is "everyone".** "Everyone who buys online" cannot be found or surveyed, and any answer fits.
- **Starting with the solution.** The team checks whether the product will be liked, without checking whether the problem exists.
- **A vague assumption.** "People will find it convenient" cannot be refuted.
- **The criterion is set afterwards.** Three agreements out of ten are declared a success in hindsight.
- **Not pivoting.** After a refutation the wording is changed, but not the customer or the problem.
- **An expensive method right away.** A service is built, although conversations with customers would have answered in a week.
