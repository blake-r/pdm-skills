# Factors for SWOT Analysis

A catalog of factor variants for SWOT. The factors are neutral dimensions, not verdicts. There are two lists, not four: the same internal factor can be a strength for one company and a weakness for another, and the same external factor can be an opportunity or a threat depending on the company and its competitors. For example, public tenders are an opportunity for a company with a procurement specialist and a threat for one without. Internal means within the company's control, external means outside it; a factor sits on one list only. The verdict is made per company, by comparing with competitors or by the effect of the trend on this company; the catalog does not give it. No source sets a fixed SWOT list. Each list has two parts: factors taken from established frameworks, then "What else may be", which the card author derived by reasoning and which no source lists. The card's rule of three to five main factors per quadrant still applies; the catalog has no cap.

## Internal factors (strength or weakness)

### From frameworks

The company's activities in Porter's value chain (see the [Value Chain and Nets card](../value-chain-and-nets/card.md)): five primary and four support activities. The question for each: how does it work against rivals?

- Inbound logistics: receiving, storing and distributing inputs, such as stock, content or data.
- Operations: turning inputs into the product or service; quality, cost and speed.
- Outbound logistics: delivering the product to the buyer; speed, accuracy, cost.
- Marketing and sales: how customers learn about us and buy; channels, price, acquisition cost.
- Service: support and after-sale service; resolution time, retention.
- Firm infrastructure: management, planning, finance, legal, quality systems.
- Human resources: hiring, skills, training, retention of people.
- Technology: product and process technology, platform, data, speed of change.
- Procurement: terms and reliability of what we buy from suppliers.

### What else may be

Product and customers:
- Range, quality, price position of the product against rivals.
- Customer base: size, loyalty, concentration in a few large customers.
- Fit of the product to a specific segment; depth of use among existing customers.
- Brand recognition and trust among target customers; reviews and reputation.
- Community, content or partner network around the product.

Finance:
- Funding: how long we can invest or absorb losses.
- Margin and unit economics against rivals.
- Debt load and working capital.
- Access to new capital: investors, banks, parent company.

Technology and data:
- Architecture: how easily it scales and changes.
- Information security and reliability.
- Customer data we hold that rivals lack, and its quality.
- Intellectual property: patents, trademarks, exclusive rights.

People and organization:
- Key skills for critical tasks, such as public procurement, licensing or machine learning.
- Leadership and its experience in this market.
- Decision speed: time from idea to launch.
- Culture: readiness to experiment, to admit mistakes, to work across teams.
- Dependence on a few key people.

Position and assets:
- Licenses, certificates and permits we already hold.
- Physical assets and locations: warehouses, offices, equipment.
- Partnerships and exclusive agreements.
- Compliance and risk management: how mature the processes are.

## External factors (opportunity or threat)

### From frameworks

Porter's five forces (see the [Porter's Five Forces card](../../business-research/porters-five-forces/card.md); Porter, "How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy", [HBR, 1979](https://hbr.org/1979/03/how-competitive-forces-shape-strategy)):
- Buyers: how easily can they switch or press the price?
- Suppliers: are their terms or concentration changing?
- New entrants: are barriers to entry falling or rising?
- Substitutes: do alternatives appear for what we offer?
- Rivalry among existing players: number, balance of strength, price and investment moves.

PESTEL's six blocks (see the [PESTEL Analysis card](../../business-research/pestel-analysis/card.md)):
- Political: policy, sanctions, trade rules, tax and tariff policy.
- Economic: growth, inflation, interest rates, exchange rates, incomes.
- Social: demographics, habits, trust, values.
- Technological: new technologies and standards, cyber threats.
- Environmental: climate and sustainability requirements.
- Legal: laws, licensing, data protection, consumer rules.

### What else may be

Market and customers:
- Market growth or shrinkage in our segment.
- Customer needs moving toward or away from our offer.
- Seasonality and demand cycles.
- Customer concentration: a few large buyers in the market.
- Price of customer acquisition in available channels, such as advertising costs.

Competitors and partners:
- Mergers and consolidation among rivals.
- Rivals' moves: price cuts, new products, entry into our segment.
- Large platforms entering our niche.
- Availability of partners, integrators and distributors.
- Open-source or free alternatives.

Platforms and infrastructure:
- Access terms to customers on platforms: app stores, marketplaces, payment systems.
- Reliability and cost of external infrastructure: cloud, payments, logistics, telecom.
- Cost and speed of delivery in the region.

Regulation and politics:
- Public and corporate tenders: are they open to a company like ours?
- Industry standards and certification requirements.
- Incentives, subsidies and state programs.
- Court practice and the speed of enforcement.

Economy and finance:
- Availability of investment and credit for our kind of business.
- Cost of imported inputs and dependence on one currency.
- Labor market: availability and cost of the specialists we need.

Society and environment:
- Changes in habits caused by a shock: pandemic, crisis, migration.
- Expectations on privacy, sustainability and social responsibility.
- Public attitude to the category and media attention.
- Natural and geopolitical risks in the regions where we operate.

## Where the groups come from

The "From frameworks" parts follow the cards named above; the grouping inside them is the cards', not a SWOT source's. Porter's 1979 article was opened and read; the value chain activities and the PESTEL blocks are taken as listed in the repo cards, whose sources are given there. The "What else may be" parts are the card author's reasoning and have no source.
