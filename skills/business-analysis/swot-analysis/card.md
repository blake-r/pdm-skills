# SWOT Analysis

## Essence

SWOT is a way to assess a company, product or option through four groups of factors:

| | Helpful | Harmful |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal** — what the company manages | S, Strengths | W, Weaknesses |
| **External** — market, competitors, regulation | O, Opportunities | T, Threats |

Behind the quadrants are only two lists of factors, internal and external; the same factor is a strength for one company and a weakness for another. The list of four quadrants alone does not produce decisions. Decisions come from the intersections: in the extended matrix (TOWS), internal factors are in the rows and external factors in the columns, and a strategy is formulated at each intersection.

## When to use and when not to

Use it when you need to quickly assemble a picture before a strategic decision: entering a market, launching a line of business, choosing between options; when a team needs to agree on what is strong and what holds it back; when you need to check whether a plan rests on real strengths.

Do not use it instead of calculations and market research: SWOT collects what is known but does not measure. It does not fit small product decisions: the factors would turn into generic words.

Limitations:

- **Factors are not weighted.** One serious threat and five minor strengths look the same until they are ranked.
- **The "internal — external" boundary gets blurred.** "Market growth" is an opportunity, not a strength; "our brand" is a strength, not an opportunity.
- **A snapshot in time.** In six months the market and the company will be different.

## Scheme

| | O · Opportunities | T · Threats |
|---|---|---|
| **S · Strengths** | SO: use strength to seize an opportunity | ST: use strength to counter a threat |
| **W · Weaknesses** | WO: use an opportunity to close a weakness | WT: reduce the weakness, avoid the threat |

## How to apply

1. **Collect the factors.** Variants for both lists are in [factors.md](factors.md). Strengths and weaknesses are stated against competitors, not in a vacuum; opportunities and threats are external, with a source.
2. **Rank:** in each quadrant, keep three to five main factors.
3. **Formulate strategies at the intersections** SO, ST, WO, WT: each rests on a specific pair of factors.
4. **Select strategies:** assess what is realistically doable and what gives the most, and turn the chosen ones into decisions with an owner and a deadline.

## Example

Ozon is a multi-category online store; in the first half of 2020 it accounted for about 6.6% of Russian online retail. In the IPO prospectus of November 2020, the company itself described its strengths, risks and market; we broke them down by quadrant.

| | O · Opportunities | T · Threats |
|---|---|---|
| **S** · leader of multi-category online retail; marketplace since September 2018 — 45% of GMV (the value of all goods sold through the platform) for 9 months of 2020; 18.1 thousand sellers provide more than 85% of the assortment | online retail share in Russia — 6.0% in 2019, 8.1% expected in 2020; the pandemic accelerated the shift to online | competitive market: Wildberries, Beru and other multi-category players |
| **W** · loss of RUB 19.4 billion in 2019 | | |

Decision: Ozon went public and announced a strategy of four directions: expand the assortment and categories, expand logistics with a focus on the regions, build an ecosystem of services around retail, and develop advertising. The link between these directions and the SO intersection — "marketplace strength plus a growing market" — is our interpretation.

Result: GMV including services was RUB 197.4 billion in 2020, 448.3 billion in 2021 and 832.2 billion in 2022; the marketplace share was 47.8%, 64.8% and 76.2%. Active sellers exceeded 90 thousand in 2021, and advertising revenue in 2022 was RUB 26.3 billion. The weakness remained: the 2022 loss was RUB 58.2 billion.

Sources of the case: Ozon Holdings, [IPO prospectus, 2020](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1822829/000119312520302121/d29162d424b4.htm); [annual report on Form 20-F for 2022](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1822829/000119312523115002/d445088d20f.htm).

## Common mistakes

- **Four lists without strategies.** The analysis ends with quadrants, and no decision is made.
- **Factor on the wrong list.** "A growing market" is listed as a strength.
- **Blurred internal-external split.** One factor stands on both lists, or an outside factor is treated as under the company's control.
- **Verdict fixed to the factor.** "Brand" is always a strength; whether it is one depends on the rivals.
- **Strengths without comparison.** "A good team" is something every competitor has.
- **Long lists without ranking.** Twenty factors in a quadrant — the main ones get lost.
- **Factors without sources.** Threats and opportunities are invented rather than taken from market data.

## Source

The predecessor of SWOT is the SOFT approach (Satisfactory, Opportunity, Fault, Threat), developed in 1965 by the TAPP research group at the Stanford Research Institute under Robert F. Stewart. The four-part combination was tested in SRI seminars in 1966 and first published in 1967 (Ansoff and Stewart) under the name "gap analysis"; the earliest document called SWOT that the authors cite is from 1972. The Harvard Business School origin is not supported by archival evidence, and no single inventor is established (Albert Humphrey, a former TAPP member, left a later account that the authors use as a lead and check against archives): R. Puyt, F. Lie, D. Madsen, [«From SOFT approach to SWOT analysis, a historical reconstruction»](https://doi.org/10.1108/JMH-05-2023-0047), Journal of Management History, 2025. The extended strategy matrix was proposed by Heinz Weihrich: [«The TOWS matrix — A tool for situational analysis»](https://doi.org/10.1016/0024-6301(82)90120-0), Long Range Planning, 1982.
