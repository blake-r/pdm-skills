# Ansoff Matrix

## Essence

The Ansoff Matrix is a 2x2 grid for choosing a growth direction. One axis is products (existing or new), the other is markets (existing or new), so a company has four ways to grow: sell more of what it has to current customers (market penetration), take current products to new customers (market development), offer new products to current customers (product development), or offer new products to new customers (diversification). The grid does not calculate anything. It makes the team name which of the four moves each growth idea is, and so how much new knowledge the idea demands and how risky it is.

## When to use and when not to

Use it when a company or a product line has to pick where growth will come from: this year's plan, a new-feature or new-country debate, a portfolio review. Inputs: the current products and markets, a list of growth ideas, and what the team knows about customers, competitors and its own capabilities.

Do not use it to size or rank the options: the grid gives no revenue, probability or payback.

Limitations:

- **The risk order is a rule of thumb.** Penetration is usually the least risky and diversification the riskiest, but a crowded market can make penetration costlier than a clean new segment.
- **"New" is a judgment.** A feature that is new to the company often brings a new customer need too, so the product-development and diversification cells blur. Fix in writing what counts as new before sorting ideas.
- **Competitors are absent.** The grid ignores how rivals will react, and it ignores the cost of disturbing the current business.
- **It sorts moves, it does not pick one.** Run a separate check on the fit and the numbers behind the chosen cell.

## Scheme

```
                 Existing products        New products
Existing  │ Market penetration     │ Product development  │
markets   │ more of the same to    │ new offer for the    │
          │ the same customers     │ same customers       │
New       │ Market development     │ Diversification      │
markets   │ same offer to new      │ new offer to new     │
          │ customers              │ customers            │
Risk grows from the top left to the bottom right
```

## How to apply

1. **Fix the unit and the horizon:** one product line or the whole company, and the planning period.
2. **Define the axes.** Write what counts as an existing product (a version, a category, a business model) and an existing market (a customer segment, a region, a channel). Keep the definitions for the whole exercise.
3. **List the growth ideas,** at least one per cell, with a source for each: customer data, research, competitor moves.
4. **Place each idea in a cell** by two questions: is the product new to the company, and are the customers new to it. Split an idea that needs both a new product and a new segment.
5. **Mark what is new in each idea:** the skills, channels, partners, regulation and investment the company does not have yet.
6. **Compare the cells.** Start from penetration and move to the right or down only when the cell above has run out: share is capped, the segment is saturated or the market is shrinking. Use the risk order as a prior, not a verdict.
7. **Choose one direction (at most two),** give an owner, a date, a metric that shows it works and a stop condition. Record why the other cells were rejected.
8. **Interpret the result.** The chosen cell tells which kind of evidence is needed next: sales data for penetration, a market study for market development, customer research and a prototype for product development, both for diversification.

## Example

Amazon, 16 June 2017. Amazon announced an agreement to buy Whole Foods Market, a natural and organic grocery chain with about $16 billion in sales in fiscal 2016 and more than 460 stores in the US, Canada and the UK. Price: $42 per share in cash, about $13.7 billion including net debt.

| | Existing products | New products |
|---|---|---|
| Existing markets | N/A | N/A |
| New markets | N/A | Buy a grocery chain: Whole Foods |

Decision, as stated in the press release: Amazon signs the merger agreement. Whole Foods keeps its brand, John Mackey stays CEO, headquarters stay in Austin. Closing was expected in the second half of 2017, subject to shareholder and regulatory approval.

What the case lacks: Amazon did not publish a matrix, so the quadrant is our classification, and the release gives no Amazon grocery figures, no rejected options and no success metric. Whether this is diversification or market development for Amazon is arguable. The tool is therefore not visible in its own form: K5=1.

Case source: Amazon, [«Amazon to Acquire Whole Foods Market»](https://press.aboutamazon.com/2017/6/amazon-to-acquire-whole-foods-market), press release, 16.06.2017.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the risk order as a law.** The grid ranks risk by how much is new; it does not measure it. Check each idea against its own evidence.
- **Counting a renamed product as new.** A new variant sold to current customers is penetration or a small product change, not diversification.
- **Placing an idea in the wrong cell by loose "new".** Define existing and new for products and markets first.
- **Skipping penetration.** Teams jump to exciting cells while share in the current market is still open.
- **Using the grid as the plan.** A cell is a direction. Without owner, metric and date nothing is decided.
- **Forgetting competitors and the core.** A move into a new cell can provoke rivals or starve the current business; the grid does not show either.
- **Mixing the unit.** Placing company-wide and product-line ideas in the same grid makes the cells incomparable.
