# ADL Matrix

## Essence

The ADL matrix (named after the consulting firm Arthur D. Little, ADL) helps choose the strategy for a business unit based on two features: the stage of the industry's life cycle (embryonic, growth, mature, aging) and the competitive position the business holds (dominant, strong, favorable, acceptable (tenable), weak). The intersection gives 20 cells, each with its own strategic direction. The Wiley reference article adds a sixth position, "non-viable" (24 cells); Think Insights and PAF count 20.

## When to use and when not to

Use it when you need to decide where to take a separate business or product line: grow share, defend, narrow to a niche, harvest profit or exit. Inputs: data about the industry and about the business's position relative to competitors.

Do not apply it to a whole company if its directions differ: stage and position are assessed separately for each business unit that could be sold and would survive on its own.

Limitations:

- **The stage does not always move forward.** Sources note that an industry does not necessarily follow a smooth S-curve: a market can be "rejuvenated", and the duration of stages varies greatly.
- **Position is an expert assessment.** In the ADL description, position is determined by a set of qualitative factors, not just market share; the assessments are subjective and each needs evidence.

## Scheme

```
                  Embryonic    Growth    Mature     Aging
Dominant              ·          ·         ·          ·
Strong                ·          ·         ·          ·
Favorable             ·          ·         ·          ·
Acceptable            ·          ·         ·          ·
Weak                  ·          ·         ·          ·
(·) — strategic direction for the cell;
Wiley: a sixth row "non-viable" — immediate exit
```

ADL action families: natural development, selective development, profit viability, withdrawal.

## How to apply

1. **Identify the business unit:** a set of products with common customers, competitors and price level.
2. **Determine the industry stage** using ADL's eight factors: growth rate, industry potential, breadth of the product line, number of competitors, stability of market shares, buyer behavior, ease of entry, technology. If the factors disagree, record the reason for the final judgment.
3. **Determine the position.** ADL assesses position with qualitative factors, not market share. Dominant is rare, close to a monopoly or a technological superiority. Strong is a clear advantage (Wiley: usually a relative share above 1.5; a guideline, not a criterion). Favorable is a unique trait: dominance in a niche, dedicated raw materials or special relationships with a sales channel. Acceptable (tenable) means one can remain in the market without a distinctive competence; survival is not in question. Weak will not hold in the long run: develop or exit. The Think Insights definitions (for example, tenable as "a niche with a limited customer base") differ from Wiley; follow Wiley.
4. **Find the cell** and choose the family of actions, then a concrete step: start up, keep share, be content with the position (growth with industry), gain position gradually or aggressively, defend, harvest profit (in Wiley it is possible at any stage; speed depends on position). The mapping of families to Wiley cells is shown only in a chart (Figure 3 in the article); there is no verbal rule there, so do not invent one.
5. **Check the decision:** risk is highest for an embryonic business with a weak position and lowest for a dominant one in a mature industry; aggressive strategies are riskier.
6. **Set a revisit date.**

## Example

The only worked e-commerce analysis found is Amazon on the Think Insights site (M. Sridharan, 27.04.2022): in the embryonic stage the emphasis was on a dominant position, investment in platform R&D and the brand; in growth, the strong position was used to expand product categories and the logistics network; in maturity, the emphasis is on innovation (cloud computing, streaming) and acquisitions (Whole Foods).

What the case does not include: references to Amazon documents, dates, figures or signs of a decision made. It is a retelling, not a published company decision. The stages are tied to the company's trajectory; industry maturity ("nearing maturity") is named without evidence. No public e-commerce or fintech case with a completed matrix and a decision made could be found.

Source of the case: [Arthur D Little Matrix](https://thinkinsights.net/strategy/arthur-d-little-matrix), Think Insights.

## Common mistakes

- **Rating the company, not the business unit.** One company has different positions in different industries.
- **Confusing industry stage with company stage.** A startup in a mature industry remains in a mature industry.
- **Overrating position.** If there is no data on an advantage for "strong", the position is at best favorable.
- **Taking the strategy from the template word for word.** The cell indicates a direction; it does not know resource constraints.
- **Treating the life cycle as inevitable.** An industry can return to growth after a technological shift.
- **Not revisiting.** Position shifts quickly.
