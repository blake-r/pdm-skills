# Technology Adoption Lifecycle

## Essence

The Technology Adoption Lifecycle is a model in which a new technology spreads among buyers in five groups: innovators (about 2.5%), early adopters (13.5%), early majority (34%), late majority (34%) and laggards (16%). The groups differ in their attitude to risk. Innovators take up the new for its novelty. Early adopters do it for a gain they see before others and tolerate imperfections. The early majority are pragmatists: they need a ready-made solution and an example of a similar company. The late majority buy when the technology has become standard; laggards buy when it is no longer possible to do without it.

Geoffrey Moore added to the model the chasm between early adopters and the early majority. Pragmatists do not trust enthusiasts' examples, so a product that is popular with the first group often gets stuck. Moore's advice: take one niche of the early majority as a beachhead and give it a whole product — everything needed for the task to be solved completely: integrations, support, a clear price.

## When to use and when not to

Use it when first customers already exist, growth has hit a wall and it is unclear why the rest are not buying. When you need to decide which segment to take next and what to add to the product for it. When sales rely on recommendations from similar customers.

Do not apply it to a product that is bought out of habit or for price, without a change in the way of working — there is no risk there that divides buyers into groups. It does not fit if the purchase is legally mandatory: the speed of adoption is set by the deadline, not by attitude to risk.

Limitations:

- **The percentages are conditional.** The group boundaries are drawn from the standard deviations of the normal distribution, not measured in every market.
- **The chasm is an observation, not a law.** Some products cross it unnoticed, others get stuck for years.
- **The group depends on the product.** The same customer can be an innovator in payments and a laggard in accounting.

## How to apply

1. **Define the technology and the market:** what exactly is being adopted and who decides on the purchase.
2. **Describe the groups in your case:** who bought first, who buys now, who does not yet, and what each one needs.
3. **Find where the product is now:** the share or number of buyers, with a date, and which group is buying.
4. **If growth has stalled before the majority,** choose one pragmatist niche as a beachhead: where the problem hurts most and where customers advise each other.
5. **Assemble a whole product for it:** what is missing for the task to be solved without further adjustments.
6. **Set by what share or number of customers in the niche** and when you will check that the beachhead is taken.

## Example

Square is an American payments company. In the 2015 IPO prospectus, Jack Dorsey described how co-founder Jim McKelvey could not accept cards for his work as an artist; banks demanded documents, credit checks and months of waiting — "the whole process was exclusionary and unfair". Square's first customers were the same small sellers: a card reader that plugged into a phone, connection at once, and a single rate of 2.75% since February 2011.

In the prospectus the company announced its next step — large sellers; in the annual reports, large sellers are those with card volume above $125 thousand a year. For them: a sales and account-management team, individual rates from November 2013, then point-of-sale solutions for retail and restaurants, the Square Register all-in-one terminal at the end of 2017 and the Square Terminal at the end of 2018.

| Q4 | 2014 | 2015 | 2016 | 2017 | 2018 | 2019 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Share of large sellers in volume | 33% | 39% | 43% | 47% | 51% | 55% |

Card volume grew from $23.8 billion in 2014 to $106.2 billion in 2019.

What the case does not include: Square did not use the model and did not talk about a chasm or a beachhead. The groups and the choice of large sellers as the next segment are our marking. The company did not pick a single beachhead niche: it developed solutions at once for retail, restaurants and services. The shares for 2016–2017 were refined in later reports.

Sources of the case: [Square S-1 prospectus](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001512673/000119312515343733/d937622ds1.htm), 2015; annual 10-K reports for [2015](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1512673/000151267316000002/a10-kfilingsquareinc2015.htm), [2016](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1512673/000162828017001754/a10-kfilingsquareinc2016.htm), [2017](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1512673/000151267318000004/a10-kfilingsquareinc2017.htm) and [2019](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1512673/000162828020002303/sq-20191231.htm).

## Common mistakes

- **Selling to the majority the same way as to enthusiasts.** Pragmatists need not novelty but a ready solution and an example of a similar customer.
- **Going into all niches at once.** None of them builds the critical mass of recommendations.
- **Treating the technology as the product.** Without integrations, support and a clear price, a pragmatist will not buy.
- **Taking early enthusiasts' success for the market.** Quick first sales say nothing about the demand of the majority.
- **Not checking the result.** The beachhead is declared, but the niche's share is not measured.
