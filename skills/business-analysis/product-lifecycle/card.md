# Product Lifecycle

## Essence

The Product Lifecycle is a model in which a product's sales pass through four stages over time: introduction, growth, maturity and decline. At introduction, sales are small and launch costs are high. At growth, sales rise quickly and competitors arrive. At maturity, growth stops and the fight is for share and price. At decline, sales and profit fall.

The model is needed not to slap on a label, but to choose a decision that fits the stage. At growth, money goes into capturing the market. At maturity, one looks for ways to extend the product's life: a new use, a new market, a different price. At decline, one decides whether to take cash from the product without new investment or to exit it.

## When to use and when not to

Use it when a product or category has several years of sales history and you need to decide where to invest next. When growth has slowed and it is unclear whether this is a temporary dip or the start of maturity. When a portfolio has several directions and one of them consumes resources without returns.

Do not apply it to a product younger than one or two years: a short series does not show the stage. It does not fit if sales depend on a single external event, such as a season or a regulator: a fluctuation is easy to mistake for a change of stage.

Limitations:

- **The stage is visible only in hindsight.** A slowdown in growth may turn out to be a one-year slump rather than the start of maturity.
- **The curve is not a law.** Some products do not decline for decades, others never grow. Dhalla and Yuspeh in 1976 called the model a poor guide for individual brands.
- **The model can fulfill itself.** The team decides that the product is in decline, cuts investment, and sales fall because of that very cut.

Next to the curve, write where the product is now and which decision follows from it.

## How to apply

1. **Choose the metric:** revenue, deal volume, number of active customers. It must be comparable year to year.
2. **Collect the series** for all years of the product from one source; mark any changes in accounting methodology.
3. **Mark the stages** by growth rate and signs: share of new customers, margin, number of competitors.
4. **Determine where the product is now** and check whether the change is explained by a one-off event.
5. **Choose the decision for the stage:** invest, extend, or take cash and exit.
6. **Set by which numbers and when** you will check whether the decision worked.

## Example

Groupon is an American marketplace of discounts on local services, later also on goods. Using its annual SEC filings, we marked the stages by the deal volume through the platform, gross billings:

| Years | Deal volume, $ | Stage, per our marking |
|---|---|---|
| 2009 | 34 million | introduction |
| 2010–2013 | 0.75 → 5.8 billion | growth |
| 2014–2017 | peak of 6.3 billion in 2015 | maturity |
| 2018–2019 | 5.2 → 4.6 billion | decline |

Active customers were 49.5 million in 2017 and 43.6 million in 2019.

In February 2020 the company announced its exit from the goods category, Goods. In the letter to shareholders: in 2019 goods received about 40% of impressions on the site, yet they generated about 20% of gross profit; the contribution of goods to gross profit had been falling for four quarters in a row. The company's conclusion: goods had outlived their role as a business driver. The decision was to focus on local services and to return deal volume growth to high single digits by 2022.

Result: goods were moved to a third-party marketplace model — in North America in 2020, and in the other countries in Q4 2021. The company did not fully exit the category. Growth did not return: deal volume fell from 4.6 billion in 2019 to 1.8 billion in 2022 and 1.6 billion in 2023, and active customers dropped to 16.5 million. Part of the decline is explained by the COVID-19 pandemic, and it cannot be separated from the effect of the decision based on the reports.

What the case does not include: Groupon did not use a lifecycle model and did not name stages. The marking is ours. The figures are as reported in each year's 10-K: the 2017 report restated 2015 from $6.3 to $5.7 billion after discontinued operations in 11 countries were excluded, so on the restated base 2015–2017 is flat rather than a sharp peak. Revenue is not comparable across years: goods were for some time recorded at full price, so deal volume was used instead.

Sources of the case: [letter to shareholders for Q4 2019](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1490281/000149028120000019/a201910kstockholderlette.htm), February 18, 2020; annual 10-K reports for [2011](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1490281/000144530512000922/groupon10-k.htm), [2015](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1490281/000149028116000087/groupon201510-k.htm), [2019](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1490281/000149028120000021/a201910-k.htm), [2021](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1490281/000149028122000027/grpn-20211231.htm) and [2023](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1490281/000149028124000024/grpn-20231231.htm).

## Common mistakes

- **Determining the stage from one year.** One weak year is declared a decline and investment stops.
- **Mixing product, category and brand.** A category may be in maturity while a single product within it is in growth.
- **The metric is not comparable between years.** A change in revenue accounting creates a false growth or decline.
- **A stage without a decision.** The curve is drawn, but investments and plans have not changed.
- **Not looking for extension at maturity.** Money is taken out immediately, although a new use or market could have restored growth.
