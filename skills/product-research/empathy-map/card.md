# Empathy Map

## Essence

The Empathy Map is a canvas on which a team describes one person: what they see, hear, say and do, and what they think and feel while doing it. The goal is to look at the product through their eyes, not through the team's.

The canvas consists of three parts:

- **goal** at the top: whom we are studying and what they need to do — the task, the decision, the expected outcome;
- **observable** around the edge: what the person sees, says, does and hears. This can be heard in an interview or seen;
- **head** in the centre: what they think and feel, their **pains** (fears, irritations) and **gains** (wants, hopes). This is not observed; it is inferred from the observable.

The canvas author Dave Gray numbers the sections to set the order of work. First go around the observable in a circle, as if walking in someone else's shoes, and only then look into the head.

## When to use and when not to

Use it during and right after interviews and observations, so that the team records what it has seen and agrees on whom the product is for. Use it when each team member pictures the user differently. Use it before generating ideas, so that ideas answer pains rather than the team's tastes.

Do not use it instead of research: a map filled from the team's memory records its guesses. It is not suitable for comparing user groups or estimating their size — it describes one person or one type, not statistics.

Limitations:

- **The centre of the canvas is a conclusion, not a fact.** Thoughts and feelings cannot be observed; each entry in the head is best backed by a quote or an action from outside.
- **One map — one type of person.** Different roles on one canvas merge into an average user who does not exist.
- **The map does not say what to do.** Decisions come at the next step, when pains and gains are compared with the product.

## How to apply

1. **Set the goal:** whom you are studying, in what situation, and what they need to do. Phrase it through behaviour that can be observed.
2. **Conduct interviews and observations** and keep the canvas in front of you: the sectors suggest what to ask and what to look at. Add call-centre records to support and reviews.
3. **Fill in the observable** in a circle: sees, says, does, hears. One thought — one sticky note, preferably with a verbatim quote.
4. **Fill in the head:** what the person thinks and feels, their pains and gains. Link each entry to something observed.
5. **Look for contradictions:** says one thing, does another. That is usually where the most value lies.
6. **Decide** which pains the product addresses, and write down how to check it.

## Example

The design agency UXDA, which works with banks, designed a concept of a mobile bank, Light Bank. Interviews, open data and social-media surveys produced three types of future clients: a millennial student, a sales manager and an entrepreneur. For each, the agency built an empathy map. The article about the project retells the client types and the conclusions from the maps:

| Sector | What is recorded |
|---|---|
| Who | energetic people who follow trends and value beautiful things |
| Hears | friends who already use neobanks advise trying them |
| Thinks and feels | want to use solutions like neobanks |
| Pains | hard to make sense of outdated design of bank services |

The agency's conclusion after the research: the design must be emotionally engaging, delightful, easy to use and innovative. The table assigns the article's lines to sectors; the article itself does not show which sector each line sits in.

Result: the concept made the shortlists of the iF Design Award 2019 and the Red Dot Award 2019.

What the case does not have: Light Bank is the agency's concept, not a working bank, so there are no clients and no metrics. The article does not retell the sectors "sees", "says" and "does" or show the maps themselves. The decision is formulated in general terms and cannot be derived directly from the maps.

Source of the case: UXDA, [«Case Study: Luxury Mobile Banking App UI Design for Emotions»](https://theuxda.com/blog/ux-design-case-study-most-beautiful-banking-in-the-world).

## Common mistakes

- **Filling it in without research.** The map repeats the team's ideas about the user.
- **Starting with the head.** The team immediately writes feelings it attributes to the person and then looks for confirmation.
- **Mixing several roles.** A buyer and a seller on one canvas produce contradictory entries.
- **Skipping the goal.** Without the task at the top, the sectors get filled with anything.
- **Stopping at the map.** The canvas hangs on the wall, but no product decision refers to it.
