# Expert Interview

## Essence

An expert interview is a way to test hypotheses not with end consumers, but with market experts: people who know consumers through their work. According to the description in PAF (Product Architecture Framework, a product architecture framework), the form is free: interview, survey, workshop, storytelling. It is a fast route to key ideas about consumers, but not to numbers.

## When to use and when not to

Use it when key ideas about consumers are needed from those who know them through work (PAF), and speed matters more than precision. Inputs: a hypothesis and a list of roles that have the needed knowledge. For questions about rules and partners the method also suits (author's conclusion).

Do not use it for shares and quantities (PAF).

Less obvious points:

- **An expert is not free of distortions.** PAF: the psychological risks for an expert are the same as for a consumer.
- **Experts are few and hard to access** (PAF), so people who are available get picked. This skews the picture (author's conclusion).
- **An expert speaks from their own position:** a bank describes the world of banks. The answer is an assessment from the standpoint of a role, not a fact about your client (author's conclusion).

## Scheme

```
Hypothesis + decision → expert roles (who knows what)
 → questions about the expert's work and barriers
 → record: expert's answer │ conclusion (marked) │ role
 → comparison of roles, disagreements
 → decision → check on consumer behaviour
```

## How to apply

1. **Write down the hypothesis and the decision** that depends on it.
2. **List the expert roles** and what each one knows; more than one role is needed (author's conclusion).
3. **Ask open questions about their real experience and barriers,** not "would they like our solution" and not hypothetical situations (general PAF advice on interviews). PAF does not name a number or a duration of expert sessions for this method.
4. **Conduct the conversation in pairs:** one leads, one records (general PAF advice on interviews). Do not interrupt; process the records immediately.
5. **Separate the answer from the conclusion:** what the expert said, and what you understood (label "author's conclusion").
6. **Compare the roles.** A disagreement is a separate result, not noise.
7. **Write down the decision and a way to check it on consumers** (author's conclusion).

## Example

Fintech: M-PESA (a payment service by Safaricom and Vodafone, Kenya). Told by Nick Hughes, a manager at Vodafone: in mid-2003 he spent several weeks preparing a grant application (the submission date is not given), and after the contracts were signed he organised open workshops in Nairobi and Dar es Salaam. Invited were banks, microfinance institutions (MFIs), technology providers, NGOs interested in microlending, and communications and finance regulators.

| Question | Authors' summary | Conclusion | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Suppose technology can do anything: what are the main difficulties in growing a business or expanding access to finance?" | Two general conclusions: (1) the best internal processes will speed up and improve the efficiency of service delivery; (2) it must be very simple for clients to get access to finance | Authors: the organisation thinks about the consumer out of habit, so they took the second conclusion | A pilot of three: operator, MFI, commercial bank; receive and repay a small loan from the phone via a network of top-up agents |

The pilot with the MFI Faulu Kenya started on 11.10.2005. It showed that clients used the service for other purposes, including sending money; launching with the MFI was considered too complicated, and the basis of the offer, "Send Money Home", was chosen at a workshop of the Safaricom commercial team. Launch: March 2007. Not in the source: the participants and their number, verbatim answers, workshop dates. Caveats: the single question is hypothetical, which goes against PAF advice; the choice of the second conclusion and the pilot design are the authors' decision (p. 68); step 6 ("compare the roles") is not confirmed by the example.

Source: N. Hughes, S. Lonie, [«M-PESA: Mobile Money for the "Unbanked"»](https://doi.org/10.1162/itgg.2007.2.1-2.63), *Innovations* 2(1–2), 2007, pp. 63–81; [PDF copy](http://www.nixdell.com/classes/Tech-for-the-underserved/m-pesa.pdf), workshops on pp. 67–68.

## Common mistakes

- **An expert instead of a consumer.** In the case, the MFI loan was the authors' plan based on the workshop results, while clients started sending money.
- **Numbers from an expert:** "70% of clients" is an eyeball estimate (PAF: the method is not for quantities).
- **One role:** only banks or only regulators.
- **Belief without allowance for position and distortions** (PAF).
- **A question about your idea** instead of their work: the answer is a polite "yes".
- **Words and conclusion merged:** it is not visible what the expert said.
- **Taking those who are available**, not those who are needed (PAF: access is hard).
- **No check on consumers.**
