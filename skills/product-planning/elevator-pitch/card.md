# Elevator Pitch

## Essence

An elevator pitch is a description of a product in the time of an elevator ride: who the customer is, what their need is, what the product is, and how it is better than the usual alternative. The goal is for the listener to grasp the idea in half a minute and be able to retell it to a third person. The wording is assembled from the positioning template of Geoffrey Moore in "Crossing the Chasm" (1991).

## When to use and when not to

Use it when an idea must be conveyed quickly, without slides. Inputs: the target customer, their need, the product category, the main alternative, and the difference from it.

Do not use it as a substitute for research: the template only packages what is already known about the customer. If a field has nothing to be filled with, that is a gap in knowledge, not in the wording.

Limitations:

- **Two versions of the template.** In retellings of the book the fields are worded differently: customers "dissatisfied with the current alternative" or customers "with a need or opportunity". Choose one and stick to it.

## Scheme

```
For [target customers]
who have [need or opportunity],
[product] is a [category]
that [key benefit].
Unlike [main alternative],
our product [main difference].
```

## How to apply

1. **Gather the facts:** who the customer is, what task they solve, what they use now.
2. **Fill in the fields.** Name the customer concretely: "everyone" is not a customer. Describe the need from the customer's side.
3. **Choose one alternative** that the customer actually compares with, even a manual method.
4. **Name one difference** that matters to the customer.
5. **Say it aloud** in one or two sentences, up to 30 seconds, without the template's connecting words.
6. **Check it on a person outside the topic:** have them retell it in their own words. If they cannot, rewrite.
7. **Decide:** keep the wording or rewrite it. If a field cannot be filled, plan research on the customer or the market.

## Example

Airbnb (then AirBed&Breakfast), a housing marketplace, first investor pitch deck. Photos of the slides were published by Business Insider (hereafter BI); the date of the presentation is not given.

The fields of Moore's template are not filled in the presentation; they are reconstructed from the slides:

- **For:** price-sensitive travelers and hosts willing to rent out their space.
- **Need:** a hotel cuts you off from the city and its culture, and there is no simple way to rent a room from a local or to become a host ("Problem").
- **Product is a:** a web platform where users rent out their space to visitors ("Solution").
- **Benefit:** save money, earn money, feel the city ("Solution").
- **Alternatives:** Craigslist (a classified ads site) and Hotels.com — these are named as the main competitors in BI's summary.
- **Spoken phrase:** the subtitle of the first slide, "Book rooms with locals, rather than hotels."

What the case does not contain:

- A one-sentence difference from the alternative: the "Competitive Advantages" slide gives six headings without text (1st to market, host incentive, list once, ease of use, profiles, design & brand); it is a list, not a difference.
- A check of the pitch on listeners and a decision made on its basis. Paul Graham, co-founder of the Y Combinator accelerator, wrote to investor Fred Wilson on 23.01.2009: "There's no reason this couldn't be as big as Ebay." (Translation: "There is no reason this could not become as big as eBay.") This is a recommendation, not a decision on the pitch; the founders did not see the letter, and Graham published it in March 2011.

Sources of the case: BI, ["Here's Airbnb's first-ever pitch deck, which helped it raise over $3 billion from investors"](https://www.businessinsider.com/airbnb-a-13-billion-dollar-startups-first-ever-pitch-deck-2011-9), originally published in 2011, updated 12.10.2021; P. Graham, ["Airbnb"](https://paulgraham.com/airbnb.html), March 2011.

## Common mistakes

- **Fields filled with general words.** "For small businesses that need efficiency" fits any product. Substitute another product: it should not fit.
- **The alternative is "nobody" or one the customer does not compare with.** The customer always does something now: a spreadsheet, a messenger, paper.
- **The template is read aloud.** The author of an elevator-pitch guide considers it too formal and too long for speech and advises using it for preparation.
- **Many differences.** The listener will remember one.
- **A benefit with no reason to choose.** "Convenient" does not say what the customer will get.
- **The pitch is not checked on a listener.**
- **The pitch is taken as proof.** A neat formulation does not confirm demand.
- **A field is missing, or the pitch does not fit into 30 seconds.**

## Source

Geoffrey A. Moore, "Crossing the Chasm", 1991 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crossing_the_Chasm)). The text of the book could not be verified: the exact wording, chapter and page are not confirmed. The template fields are taken from retellings: the variant "who (need or opportunity)" — [MIT CSAIL](https://groups.csail.mit.edu/mac/users/hal/misc/elevator.html); the variant "dissatisfied with" and criticism of the template — [Elevator Pitch Essentials](https://www.elevatorpitchessentials.com/essays/CrossingTheChasmElevatorPitchTemplate.html); the six fields — [Actuation Consulting](https://actuationconsulting.com/crafting-positioning-statement/).

PAF (Product Architecture Framework, Sergey Tikhomirov, productframework.ru, CC BY-SA 4.0, no date) gives only a brief definition: a description of the product "in several sentences" and "in one sentence" ([positioning](https://productframework.ru/activities/competitive_positioning_design), [concept](https://productframework.ru/product_concept)). The structure of the pitch here is taken from Moore, not from PAF.

Steps 5–6, the check by substituting another product, and the list of mistakes are conclusions of the card's author; Moore does not have them.
