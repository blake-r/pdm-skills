# Porter's Five Forces

## Essence

Porter's five forces model assesses the profitability of an industry: how much profit remains with its participants. Profit is determined by five forces: buyers, suppliers, potential new entrants, substitutes, and rivalry among existing players. The weaker the forces, the more profit; the stronger they are, the more value goes to buyers, suppliers or the fight itself.

## When to use and when not to

Use it before entering a market or niche, and when revising strategy: to understand the cause of low margins and to find a position where the pressure is weaker. You need: a defined industry, the players, prices, customers' switching costs.

Do not use it as an assessment of a single company: the model describes the structure of the industry, and a firm's result also depends on its position.

Limitations:

- **The industry boundaries determine the answer.** Narrow boundaries hide substitutes, wide ones average out segments.
- **Industry growth does not mean profitability.** The HBR summary of the 2008 article explains why a fast-growing industry is not always profitable.
- **The assessment is valid as of the analysis date.** Laws and technologies change the forces.
- **In platforms, roles are blurred.** In payments it is hard to separate buyers from suppliers (Karen Webster).

## How to apply

1. **Define the industry:** the product, geography, customer segments, and the list of players.
2. **For each force, assess its factors** (description by the Institute for Strategy and Competitiveness at Harvard Business School, HBS):
   - **new entrants:** entry barriers (economies of scale, brand costs, access to channels, government restrictions) and the capabilities of likely entrants (for example, established firms from other regions);
   - **suppliers:** only one or two suppliers of an essential input, costly or slow switching between suppliers;
   - **buyers:** large relative to sellers, the product is undifferentiated and a significant cost to them, switching is cheap, different buyer segments have different power;
   - **substitutes:** meet the same need differently (a video call instead of a trip), a favorable price-to-quality ratio, cheap switching;
   - **rivalry:** numerous or roughly equal players, slow growth, high fixed costs, high exit barriers, rivals strongly committed, differing goals.
3. **Rate the strength on the scale** weak / medium / strong and support it with a fact: share, number of players, switching cost. The scale is set by the analyst. An assessment without facts cannot be checked.
4. **Draw the conclusion:** the industry is attractive if few forces are strong; the strongest one limits profit.
5. **Assess the dynamics:** what will change the force (a law, a technology, a player's move).
6. **Make a decision:** whether to enter, which position to take, how to weaken a force (reduce dependence on a supplier, raise customers' switching cost).

## Example

The US payments industry. Karen Webster (PYMNTS, 17.03.2014) analyzed it before a session with Porter. In her analysis the card networks (American Express, MasterCard, Visa, Discover) are suppliers of the "rails" (payment infrastructure), and the buyers are merchants and issuers (banks that issue cards).

| Force | Level (our estimate from the article) | Facts from the article |
|---|---|---|
| Suppliers | Strong | Only four networks in total; universal card acceptance is long and costly to replicate |
| Buyers | Medium, growing | Large merchants negotiate terms; the Durbin Amendment cut banks' debit fees; Chase created ChaseNet |
| Entrants | Medium, barriers fell | Upheaval began around 2005: internet, mobile, cloud, regulation, the 2008 crisis; clearXchange (a network of four large banks) is a possible new supplier |
| Substitutes | Weak | Wallets run on the same rails; threat: MCX (Merchant Customer Exchange, a coalition of merchants) on the ACH network (automated clearing house) and bitcoin |
| Rivalry | Medium, growing | Issuers compete for customers; Square, ChaseNet |

The author's conclusion: the power of suppliers has barely changed, and over ten years it is unlikely to be weakened. A player's move: in August 2012 Discover licensed the PayPal network.

What the case lacks: the company's decision based on the table, and numerical estimates; the analysis is journalistic. The levels and the table are our reconstruction from the text of the article.

Case: K. Webster, [«Michael Porter's Five Forces And Payments Innovation»](https://www.pymnts.com/news/2014/a-look-at-how-e-payments-got-to-where-it-is-toda/), PYMNTS, 17.03.2014.

## Common mistakes

- **Looking only at direct competitors.** Porter (2008): competition is understood too narrowly; the fight for profit runs across all five forces.
- **Confusing a substitute with a competitor.** A substitute meets the need in a different way; in the case, wallets on the networks' rails are not a substitute.
- **Wrong boundaries and one estimate for all buyers.** The forces are counted for "the market in general", without segments.
- **Taking industry structure for the firm's destiny.** Profit is set by the structure and by the position.
- **A snapshot instead of dynamics.** The Durbin Amendment shifted the power from banks to merchants.
- **Estimates without facts.** "Strong" cannot be checked without the number of players.
- **No decision.** Without a choice, the table changes nothing.
