# Competitor Analysis Template

## Essence

The competitor analysis template is a single profile of one market player: business model, scale, product, prices, users, offer and customer reviews. Identical fields allow comparing competitors with each other and with your product: where they are strong and weak, how they differ, and where your place is. In the Product Architecture Framework (PAF), the template "is not exhaustive, but helps focus on what matters": items can be removed or added to suit the market.

## When to use and when not to

Use it before entering a market, launching a major feature, or revising prices or positioning. Inputs: competitors and open data — website, tariffs, app stores, reviews, financial reports.

Do not use it until competitors are defined. PAF distinguishes four types: direct (same problem, same way), indirect (same problem, other way), displacers (compete for the customer's money or time) and channel competitors (compete for access to customers).

Limitations:

- **Revenue, growth and market share data are usually closed.** Mark them as "estimate" with reasoning or "no data", not as a plausible figure.
- **A competitor's website is its own claim, not reality.** Check it against reviews and trial use.
- **The template describes but does not predict.** It does not contain the competitor's goals and its view of the market. Michael Porter adds these to the competitor's goals and assumptions: without them, it is hard to predict the response to your move.

## Scheme

| Block | What it contains |
|---|---|
| Business model | mission, segments, value propositions, differentiators, channels, customer relationships, partners, monetization, awards |
| Market scale | audience, territory, growth of customers and revenue, market share, investments, employees, year founded |
| Contacts | website, social networks, newsletter, knowledge base and cases |
| Product | category, place in ratings, integrations, development directions, localization |
| Competition | main, other, indirect competitors |
| Pricing and sales | monetization model, sales process, features by stage of the customer path (acquisition, activation, revenue, retention, referral) |
| Users | significant parameters of the target audience |
| Offer | what changes in the customer's life, how it is packaged, features, key screens |
| Reviews | what they praise, what they criticize, what is missing |


## How to apply

1. **Formulate the decision** the data is collected for: whether to enter a niche, what price to set. Remove the blocks that are not needed.
2. **Choose competitors** of all four types; start with the main one.
3. **Collect data by block** from open sources: website, tariffs, app stores, reviews on aggregators and forums, reports, job postings, trial use.
4. **For each field, record the source and the date,** and mark it as fact, estimate or no data. Do not close gaps with guesses.
5. **Capture the key screens** (sign-up, onboarding, payment) and note the recurring praise and complaints from reviews: repetition matters more than single vivid cases.
6. **Consolidate the profiles into one table** and answer: what is strong for them; what is weak and whether customers complain; what they do not look at; where our place is.
7. **Record the decision** and the review date: the market changes, and the profile becomes outdated.

## Example

No public case with a completed competitor template from e-commerce or fintech could be found; below is the closest real one. Maxim Evstratov describes Konstantin, who developed an online grocery store (Products Moscow meetup, 20.04.2017). He placed trial orders with competitors and counted the screens before the "Pay for order" button, assessed whether a store is passive (only informs) or interactive (collects data and speeds up choice), built a table of about 50 parameters and grouped them into clusters. The goal was a perception map: how buyers may perceive stores, which clusters competitors are concentrated in, and where competition is lower.

What the case does not contain: competitor names, parameter values, prices, reviews, the table itself, or a decision. It is a method of data collection.

Case source: M. Evstratov, [«Analyzing and comparing your IT product with competitors' products»](https://habr.com/en/articles/327296), Habr, 26.04.2017.

## Common mistakes

- **Comparing only features.** Prices, sales, channels and reviews are left out: the reasons for the competitor's success are not visible.
- **Data without a source and date.** Later it is impossible to check what is outdated.
- **Inventing what is unavailable.** A market share made up from nothing is worse than an honest "no data".
- **Copying strengths.** The analysis is needed to find what the competitor does badly or does not do.
- **Taking only direct competitors.** A displacer or an adjacent channel takes customers, but never made it into the table.
- **Doing it once and for all.** Tariffs, features and ratings change: set a review date.

## Source

The competitor analysis template is the Competitors Analysis section of PAF (Sergey Tikhomirov, productframework.ru, CC BY-SA 4.0, undated): [Competitors Analysis page](https://productframework.ru/activities/competitors_analysis). Competitor types are described on the same page, in the subsection on the competition map; "displacers" is a PAF term, not Porter's. The template on the PAF page is given as a list of fields, not as a file; the blocks are taken from that list. The four components of competitor analysis (goals, assumptions, current strategy, capabilities) are from Michael E. Porter, a 1980 book (Free Press), chapter 3 "A Framework for Competitor Analysis"; a short summary: [Four Corners Analysis](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/strategic-management/four-corners-analysis). Example: Evstratov, Habr, 2017 (link above).
