# Customer Journey Map

## Essence

A customer journey map (Customer Journey Map, CJM) is a table of how a customer moves toward their goal through contact with the company: from the first thought of buying to a repeat contact. Horizontally — the stages of the path; vertically — the slices: what the customer wants at the stage, where and how they meet the company, what they experience along the way, and what follows from it for the product. The map brings together the data of different departments and shows where the customer gets stuck or leaves.

## When to use and when not to

Use it when the customer's path goes through several steps and channels (website, app, store, support, delivery) and nobody sees it as a whole; when customers leave for unclear reasons; when you must choose which stage to improve first. Do not use it when the interaction is a single action in a single channel: there it is enough to analyze the screen or step itself.

Inputs: interviews, surveys, reviews, support contacts, step-by-step analytics, observations at points of sale.

Limits:

- **A map drawn from one's head misleads.** If the stages and emotions are invented by the team, the map confirms its assumptions instead of showing the customer.
- **One map — one customer type and one scenario.** An averaged map of new and returning customers describes neither.
- **A map does not measure.** It shows where to look for the problem; its size will be shown by behavioral data.
- **Early leavers are poorly heard.** Interviews and reviews mostly come from those who reached the purchase.
- **The bottleneck moves.** After one stage is fixed, another becomes the main problem, so the map is updated.

## Scheme

A table: columns are the stages of the path (usually 3–5), rows are the four slices.

| Slice | What is recorded at each stage |
|---|---|
| **Goals & expectations** | what the customer wants to achieve and what they expect from the company |
| **Touchpoints & scenarios** | where they meet the company and what they do |
| **Experience & problems** | what they feel and think, where it is hard for them — with a reference to data |
| **Insights & opportunities** | what follows from this and what can be improved |

Rows with stage metrics and company processes are often added.

## How to apply

1. **Choose the customer and the scenario:** who goes the path and toward which goal.
2. **Set the stages** from the customer's point of view, not the company's departments.
3. **Collect data** for each stage.
4. **Fill in the slices** for each stage; each problem gets a source.
5. **Find the bottlenecks:** where customers leave or experience the most difficulty.
6. **Choose one opportunity** and test it on part of the customers with a measurement.
7. **Update the map** after changes: the bottleneck may have moved.

## Example

Birkenstock.ru wanted to find out why customers leave the site without ordering. The team studied requests on marketplaces and in the call center, ran interviews in retail, observed behavior on the site and surveyed visitors, then built a journey map. The company did not publish the map; below it is reconstructed from two Mindbox articles.

| | Awareness | Decision | Purchase | Delivery |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Goals & expectations** | "buy beautiful and comfortable shoes" | choose where to buy, the brand and model; find the size | order on the site or buy in a store | no data |
| **Touchpoints** | no data | site with a size guide in the product card, Lamoda, Wildberries | no data | no data |
| **Experience & problems** | no data | many leave without ordering: they cannot decide on the size | no data | difficulties (reviews) |
| **Opportunities** | no data | a new size guide | no data | no data |

Decision: size was chosen as the main bottleneck. The new guide was first tested in the welcome email chain and the abandoned-cart emails, with a survey added to each email asking whether the size had been found.

Result: 95% of respondents found their size, and there were no extra calls to the call center. The guide was moved to the site; later, according to the company, the bottleneck shifted to another stage.

Sources of the case: [Mindbox, Birkenstock case](https://mindbox.ru/journal/cases/birkenstock/) (September 27, 2021), [Mindbox, "CJM on the example of Birkenstock and Okko"](https://mindbox.ru/journal/education/sostavlenie-cjm-instrukciya-i-sovety/) (January 7, 2026).

## Common mistakes

- **A map by org structure.** Stages are named after departments ("marketing", "logistics") rather than what the customer does.
- **Emotions without data.** The experience row is filled with the team's guesses, not the customers' words.
- **A map for the sake of a map.** It is not decided which stage to improve or how to check the result.
- **A one-off map.** It is not updated, and nobody notices that the bottleneck has moved.

## Source

The method has no single author: customer journey maps came from UX design (user experience design) and service design. Chris Risdon of Adaptive Path wrote in November 2011 that experience maps "have become more prominent over the past few years" and broke down the anatomy of a map for Rail Europe in [«The Anatomy of an Experience Map»](https://web.archive.org/web/20120908085140/http://adaptivepath.com/ideas/the-anatomy-of-an-experience-map). The [Product Architecture Framework](https://productframework.ru/activities/customer_research) by Sergey Tikhomirov (CC BY-SA 4.0) describes the map as the customer's path through all touchpoints and lists common slices (touchpoint, customer goals, stage metrics, company processes, actions, emotions, problems, expectations, observations). The four rows in this card group those slices; the grouping is this card's, and its author and year are not known.

## See also

- Preceded by Customer Profile: Pick one customer type from the profile; a map covers one type and scenario.
- Enhanced by Feedback Matrix: Fill the experience and problems row from reviews sorted in the feedback matrix.
- Followed by Service Blueprint: Use a service blueprint to show what staff and systems do under each step.
- Followed by Sales Funnel: Turn the journey's steps into funnel stages that each have a recorded event.
- Enhanced by AARRR Metrics: Use AARRR to find the leaking stage, then map its journey to find the cause.
- Preceded by Buyer Persona: Build the journey from the decision path the persona has described.
- Followed by User Flow: Draw the user flow for one goal inside one product after mapping the whole path.
