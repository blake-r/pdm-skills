# Forces of Progress

## Essence

Forces of Progress is a model of why a customer changes a familiar solution for a new one or stays with the old one. Four forces act on the decision. Two push toward change: dissatisfaction with the current situation (Push) and the pull of the new solution (Pull). Two hold back: habit toward the current one (Habit) and anxiety about the new one (Anxiety). A customer switches only when Push and Pull are stronger than Habit and Anxiety. That is why it is not enough for a product to be better: one must also remove the fear of change and the cost of giving up the familiar.

## When to use and when not to

Use it when customers do not move to you even though your offer is more advantageous; when a new feature does not replace the old way; when you need to understand why customers leave for a competitor. Do not use it when there is no concrete switching event or a refusal to switch, and when you need to estimate the size of demand in numbers.

Inputs: interviews or surveys of customers who recently switched, reconsidered or stayed — what did not suit them, what attracted them, what held them back, what they feared; where possible, the shares of customers with each factor.

Limits:

- **The forces do not add up to a number.** The model shows direction, but does not say how much pull exceeds anxiety; that requires measuring behavior.
- **Holding forces are poorly visible.** Customers readily talk about benefits and rarely about fear and habit.
- **Answers after the fact.** People explain past decisions more logically than they made them; interviews should be built around specific events, not opinions.

## Scheme

On the left is the current solution, on the right the new one. Above the arrow are the forces that push toward the new one; below it are the forces that pull back.

| | Force | Question |
|---|---|---|
| **Toward change** | Push of the current situation | What does not suit them about the way things are now? |
| **Toward change** | Pull of the new solution | What does the new one promise, and why is that valuable? |
| **Against change** | Habit toward the current | What would they have to give up, what are they used to? |
| **Against change** | Anxiety about the new | What are they afraid of: that it will not work, that they will lose something, that it will be hard? |

## How to apply

1. **Define the switch:** what the customer moves from and to.
2. **Collect the stories** of customers who switched, nearly switched and stayed.
3. **List the factors** under the four forces, each with a reference to its source and, if available, the share of customers.
4. **Assess the balance:** which force is the weakest "for" and which is the strongest "against".
5. **Choose what to move:** it is often cheaper to reduce anxiety or habit than to strengthen the benefit.
6. **Record the decision and the measurement:** what share of customers should switch and when you will check.

## Example

The UK Competition and Markets Authority (CMA), in 2016, concluded its investigation of the retail banking market and analyzed why customers almost never change their current account bank. The CMA did not use the forces model: below, the conclusions of the report summary are laid out by force for this card.

| Force | CMA finding (summary paragraph) |
|---|---|
| Push — weak | more than half of customers are "very satisfied" with their bank and more than a third are "fairly satisfied", although many pay above-average fees for below-average service (54, 70b) |
| Pull — weak | many think switching brings little benefit, although about 90% of customers on standard and reward accounts would on average gain about £92 a year (57–58, 70c) |
| Habit — strong | the account has no end date and no reason to review it; more than half of customers have been with their bank for over 10 years, more than a third for over 20 (65, 70a) |
| Anxiety — strong | more than half consider switching a hassle, more than 40% fear that "something will go wrong"; 51% had heard of the CASS account-switching guarantee service (FCA 2015 study) (71) |

Result: 3% of customers changed banks over one year, and 8% over three years.

The CMA's measures map onto the forces as follows: open banking (the bank, with the customer's consent, passes their data to third-party services) and the publication of service quality information (based on customers' willingness to recommend their bank) make the benefit visible; reminders to review the account give a reason that habit does not provide; against anxiety — oversight of CASS, a campaign about it, extending the redirection of payments beyond 36 months, and access to the transaction history of a closed account.

Sources of the case: [CMA report summary (August 9, 2016)](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/57a8c0fb40f0b608a7000002/summary-of-final-report-retail-banking-investigation.pdf), [full report](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/57ac9667e5274a0f6c00007a/retail-banking-market-investigation-full-final-report.pdf).

## Common mistakes

- **Benefit only.** The team strengthens the pull while customers are held back by the fear of losing payments or time.
- **A list without a balance.** Factors are written out, but it is not decided which force to move.
- **Facts instead of feelings.** A force acts only if the customer feels it: an overpayment the customer does not notice does not push them to switch. Factors are collected from customers, not invented by the team.
- **One group of customers.** Only those who switched are asked, and what held back the rest is not seen.
