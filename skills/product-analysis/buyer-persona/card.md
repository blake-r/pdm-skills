# Buyer Persona

## Essence

A buyer persona describes the typical person who chooses and buys a product, and emphasizes how the decision is made: what matters to them, what holds them back, and who takes part in the approval. It is a story about the decision: why the buyer started looking, how they compared options, and why they chose you, a competitor, or stayed as they were. The method builds the offer, the messages and the work of sales on the buyer's words, not on the team's guesses.

## When to use and when not to

Use it when the product is bought deliberately, with choice and comparison: expensive purchases, sales to companies (B2B), financial products. You need 6–10 interviews with those who made the decision in the last few months, including those who chose a competitor or refused to buy.

Do not use it:

- **Purchases made without thinking.** Adele Revella: an impulsive consumer purchase has almost no history of choice, and a persona will tell you little.
- **Instead of interviews.** Substituting sales reps' answers for conversations with buyers means getting the team's opinion.

## Scheme

Eight blocks of description in PAF (Product Architecture Framework), with a question for each:

| Block | Question for the buyer |
|---|---|
| Company goals | What result does the organization need? |
| Personal motivation | What will the choice personally give them? |
| Management motivation | What matters to the one who signs? |
| Decision criteria | What do they compare options by? |
| Barriers to purchase | What makes them doubt or refuse? |
| Approval process | Who takes part, and at which steps? |
| Budget | Which budget, and who owns it? |
| Alternatives | What will they buy instead, or will they buy nothing? |

## How to apply

1. **Choose the buyer.** One segment and one role: the one who starts the search or decides.
2. **Find 6–10 recent buyers.** Take different ones: yours, those who left for competitors, those who bought nothing.
3. **Hold 30-minute interviews.** The questions are open: "What happened on the day you decided to look?", "How did you narrow the options?", "Who else took part?". Probe general answers: "wanted to grow" — "how would that help you grow faster?".
4. **Write down the buyer's words verbatim** and mark the places where they became animated.
5. **Sort the answers into the eight blocks.** Take three to five main criteria, clarifying the meaning of each.
6. **Check repeatability.** After about six interviews the answers start to repeat; diverging roles are different personas.
7. **Decide:** which messages to put forward, which barriers to remove, what to give sales.

## Example

Deloitte Private, the unit of Deloitte Consulting for family and small business, commissioned a marketing plan from the agency Aamplify (Samuel Williams). They interviewed people who had recently chosen solutions like this one and modeled their decision, including a comparison with competitors. They found three reasons to come: help with global business growth opportunities, help with specific tasks, and technology for improving processes. Decision: Deloitte Private placed these messages at the center of its home page, and showed sales how to tell clients the same stories.

What the case lacks: the number of interviews, barriers, criteria and the approval process, and result figures ("strong results" without numbers). It is consulting, not e-commerce or fintech: no public fintech case with a filled-in form could be found.

Source of the case: A. Revella, [«Deloitte Case Study is a Powerful Endorsement for Buyer Personas»](https://buyerpersona.com/blog/deloitte-case-study-is-a-powerful-endorsement-for-buyer-personas), Buyer Persona Institute.

## Common mistakes

- **A portrait without a decision.** Age, city and hobbies describe a person but do not explain the choice.
- **Sales reps' answers instead of interviews.** Revella cites the idea of "skipping the interviews and letting sales fill in the rings" as a typical failure of teams, and compares it to an investor who cuts in half the capital the project needs.
- **Deriving from your own product.** Pain and benefit are easy to derive from the product's capabilities; the record must be written from the buyer's position and circumstances.
- **Assuming the top person decides everything.** Revella: those at the top often have less influence than sales people think. Ask who influenced each step.
- **Taking only your own customers.** Those who refused or left for competitors show barriers that those who chose you do not have.
- **Generalities in the blocks.** "Quality and price" is not a criterion; work out how the buyer checks "quality".
- **One-off research.** The buyer's circumstances change.

## Source

The idea of a persona as an archetype of the user described from data — Alan Cooper, "The Inmates Are Running the Asylum", Sams, 1999, first edition (personas for designing interfaces; [Wikipedia gives 1998](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Cooper_(software_designer))). For the buyer — Adele Revella, "Buyer Personas", Wiley, 2015 ([Open Library](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL31058304M)); Buyer Persona Institute, ["Buyer Persona Manifesto", 2nd ed., 2016](https://www.buyerpersona.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/05/Buyer-Persona-Manifesto-2016.pdf) and [Buyer Persona Research](https://buyerpersona.com/buyer-persona-research): five rings (Priority Initiatives, Success Factors, Perceived Barriers, Decision Criteria, Buyer's Journey), interviews with recent buyers.

The eight-block form is a description in PAF (Sergey Tikhomirov, productframework.ru, CC BY-SA 4.0, undated). PAF gives only the list of blocks without a source; the mapping of blocks to Revella's rings is the card author's assessment, not a claim of PAF or Revella: company goals — Priority Initiatives; personal motivation and management motivation — Success Factors; criteria — Decision Criteria; barriers — Perceived Barriers; approval — Buyer's Journey; budget and alternatives — no direct ring.
