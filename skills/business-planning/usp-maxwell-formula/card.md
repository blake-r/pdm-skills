# Maxwell USP Formula

## Essence

A USP (unique selling proposition) is a short phrase in which the customer sees what they will get and why they should choose exactly you. The formula that PAF attributes to Dane Maxwell builds such a phrase from three parts: the end result, the time to get it, and the answer to the customer's main objection. The result is a promise that can be checked.

## When to use and when not to

Use it when you need wording for a page headline, an ad or an email, and you know what the customer wants and what they fear. Inputs: the desired result in the customer's eyes, a realistic time frame, the most frequent objection.

Do not use it if the time frame cannot be kept or the result depends on the customer: an unfulfillable promise undermines trust.

Limits:

- **A source of objections is needed.** The formula does not say which objection is the main one: it is taken from conversations with customers, not invented.
- **A guarantee costs money.** Compensation ("free if we are late") is calculated from the real share of failures.

## Scheme

```
[End result]  +  [Time to get it]  +  [Answer to the objection]
 what the customer wants   how long, in numbers   "without …" or "otherwise free"
```

The template file adds "phrase", "risk", "check" and "decision" to the three blocks.

## How to apply

1. **Write the result** in the customer's words: not "a delivery service" but "the groceries I wanted".
2. **Name the time** as a number (minutes, days) and check against data what share of cases you actually meet it.
3. **Find the main objection:** what stops the customer from believing the promise or buying. Take it from interviews, support requests and reviews.
4. **Answer the objection** in one of two ways: remove the unpleasant part ("no calls and no rejections") or take the risk on yourself (a guarantee, a refund of the fee).
5. **Assemble the phrase** into one headline and read it through the customer's eyes.
6. **Decide what you accept:** whether you keep the time frame and pay under the guarantee. Record the guarantee's terms and track the share of triggered payouts.

## Example

Checkers Sixty60 (Shoprite Group, South Africa), grocery delivery from an app. A phrase in the form of the formula, reconstructed from the press release:

| Part | What it says |
|---|---|
| Result | groceries the customer wants |
| Time | delivery in 60 minutes, fee 35 rand (R35) |
| Difficulty from the release | traffic jams during power cuts, rain |
| Answer (our conclusion: to the doubt that 60 minutes can be kept) | delivery is free if the order arrives more than 30 minutes after the estimated time or contains less than 80% of the first-choice items |

Decision: on 27 March 2023 Sixty60 introduced a service guarantee. The R35 fee is returned to the app wallet and is spent on the next orders within a week of the original one. Chief strategy officer Neil Schreuder names the difficulty (60 minutes is sometimes hard to keep because of traffic and rain) and the goal (confidence in any weather), but does not write that the guarantee was introduced for this. The release does not name a customer objection. The campaign slogan in the video description: "It's great, or it's free".

Not in the case: the share of late deliveries and triggered guarantees, the cost of the guarantee, the result after launch. The company does not mention Maxwell's formula: the breakdown by parts is ours. The guarantee terms are given from the release; the full terms of the service have not been verified.

Source: press release of Shoprite Holdings, ["Checkers Sixty60 launches new delivery guarantee"](https://shopriteholdings.co.za/newsroom/2023/sixty60-service-guarantee.html), 27.03.2023.

## Common mistakes

- **A result without a time, or a time without a result.** "Fast delivery" is not a promise.
- **An invented objection.** An answer to a non-existent fear adds no trust.
- **The time is taken from the best day.** If the promise is broken in bad weather, guarantee payouts for failures become expensive; calculate by the worst conditions.
- **A blurred guarantee.** Conditions in small print ("provided that…") turn the answer to the objection into a new objection.
- **The cost of the guarantee is not calculated.** Without the share of failures, the promise "otherwise free" may turn out to be unprofitable.
- **Three parts glued into one long phrase.** Shorten one of the parts.
- **No check after launch.** Without counting guarantee triggers, it is unclear whether the promise holds.

## Source

The formula in the PAF description (Sergey Tikhomirov, productframework.ru, CC BY-SA 4.0, undated): "end result + time to get it + counterargument to the objection"; the PAF example is pizza in 30 minutes, "if we are late, it is yours for free".

The primary source is Dane Maxwell, an entrepreneur, author of the book "Start From Zero" (2020) and of a copywriting course on Mixergy (undated page; judging by the image path, around 2011). The formula is shown as an image on [the Mixergy page](https://mixergy.com/tip-copywriting-course/) (the page author is Ryan W. Johnson): "End Result Customer Wants + Specific Period of Time + Address the Objections"; the same page has Maxwell's headline "Recruit 2 Top Producing Agents Each Week Without Cold Calling Or Rejection".
