# RAPID Decision Rights

## Essence

RAPID is a tool from the consulting firm Bain & Company that states who does what in one decision. It names five roles: Recommend, Agree, Perform, Input and Decide. Each role has a named holder, and exactly one person holds the "D": the single point of accountability who commits the organization to action. Its purpose is to remove the stalls that appear when several people or units think they own the same decision, or nobody does. The result is a short table, one column per role, that everyone involved can read in a minute.

## When to use and when not to

Use it when a decision crosses several units or stakeholders and keeps stalling: a launch needing product, legal, engineering and a regional office; a pricing change; a vendor choice. Bain names four typical bottlenecks: global versus local, center versus business unit, function versus function, inside versus outside partners.

Inputs: the decision stated in one sentence, the list of people and units touched by it, and any mandatory requirements (law, regulation, security).

Do not use it for every decision. Bain says explicit RAPID roles are not worth the effort for each decision; start with high-value or high-frequency ones.

Limitations:

- **It does not make anyone decisive.** The authors: "an indecisive decision maker can ruin any good system".
- **The letters are not a sequence.** The roles are not carried out in the order of the acronym.
- **Roles can overlap.** One person can hold Perform and Input; a group can be the Decide holder only if the rule for disagreement is fixed in advance.
- **One role per decision, not per person.** A product manager can recommend in one decision and only give input in another; assign roles decision by decision.

## Scheme

```
Decision: one sentence
R Recommend - one person: gathers input, proposes
A Agree     - few, only mandatory: legal, affected unit head
P Perform   - who carries it out
I Input     - who supplies facts, affected people
D Decide    - one person: final call, owns the outcome
Rule: Recommend and Agree cannot agree -> Decide settles it
```

## How to apply

1. **State the decision** in one sentence with a deadline, so that the roles belong to one decision, not to a topic.
2. **Name the Decide holder first:** one person, close to where the decision will be carried out, with a view of the trade-offs and the resources. If a group must decide, write how a tie is broken (majority vote, or the chair's final say).
3. **Name one Recommend holder** the Decide holder trusts, who can engage everyone else quickly. Agree with the decider on context, timing, criteria and needed inputs.
4. **Assign Agree sparingly,** only for mandatory requirements or the head of a strongly affected unit. Write what the agreer checks. A veto must come with an alternative or go to the decider.
5. **Name Perform early,** and add them to Input too: they flag implementation problems and buy in sooner.
6. **List Input:** experts and people affected. Facts, not opinions. Tell them that input is weighed, not always followed.
7. **Fix the conflict rule and check the table:** one R, one D, no empty cell for D. If you cannot name the D, the decision belongs one level higher.
8. **Read the result:** the authors' sign of progress is that managers spend less time in meetings wondering why they are there. If the decision still stalls, look at the decider: no table fixes an indecisive one.

## Example

Case: Wyeth Pharmaceuticals, as told by Paul Rogers and Marcia Blenko. Wyeth wanted a leading position with a new drug, Enbrel. Competitors worked on the same class of drug, so Wyeth had to expand production capacity quickly by building a plant in Ireland. Input used to filter up through overlapping committees, slower than the competition required. Executives turned to RAPID.

The roles as the article gives them:

| Role | Holder |
|---|---|
| Recommend | people best suited to propose or offer alternatives; some responsibilities pushed down to the business units, where knowledge was greatest |
| Agree | Wyeth's top executives, who kept veto power over many important proposals |
| Perform | the business unit |
| Input | other managers |
| Decide | Cavan Redmond, executive vice president and general manager of the biotech division, for many Enbrel decisions |

Decision rule: a veto holder must offer an alternative or escalate to the person with the "D".

What the case lacks (K5=1): the article is a short narrative, not a filled form. It names no individual recommenders, no list of separate decisions, no dates and no result: it does not say that the plant was approved or how much faster decisions became. The table above is assembled from its sentences without additions. Wyeth is a pharmaceutical company, not e-commerce or fintech; no public RAPID case from those industries was found.

Case source: [Who has the "D"?](https://www.bain.com/insights/who-has-the-d/), Bain & Company, 28.06.2006.

## Common mistakes

- **Several deciders.** A "D" shared by a committee with no tie rule recreates the stall. Bain: ideally one decider per decision.
- **Agree as a veto for all.** Too many agreers turn RAPID into consensus, which the authors call a path to lowest-common-denominator compromise.
- **Reading the letters as steps.** The roles overlap in time: Perform and Input start before the decision.
- **Dropping Perform and Input.** A decision handed over without them is executed slowly and its risks stay hidden.
- **Recommender the decider does not trust.** Bain requires the decider's trust and credibility with all other roles.
- **A table nobody saw.** Roles written in a private file do not change who attends the meeting; share it.
- **Applying it to every decision.** The effort pays off on high-value or high-frequency decisions only.
