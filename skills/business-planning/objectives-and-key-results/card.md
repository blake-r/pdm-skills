# Objectives and Key Results (OKR)

## Essence

OKR is a way of setting goals and aligning them across the company, departments, teams and employees. Each goal consists of two parts:

- **Objective** — a qualitative statement of what we want to achieve; clear without explanation and inspiring.
- **Key Results** — measurable outcomes that show the objective has been achieved. Not tasks but results: a number or a milestone.

Under the key results are the actions that should move them. Goals are set for a half-year, a quarter or a year and graded at the end on a scale from 0 to 1.0. Lower-level goals support higher-level ones, and all OKRs are visible across the company.

## When to use and when not to

Use it when a company needs to choose a few main priorities for a period and tie team work to them; when teams pull in different directions; when you need to show what the company is not investing in right now.

Do not use it as a list of all team tasks or as an assessment of employees: Google explicitly separates OKRs from employee evaluation, otherwise an aspirational goal graded 0.7 looks like a failure. It is not suitable when priorities cannot be agreed even for a quarter.

Limits:

- **A number is not a goal.** A key result can be met formally without moving the objective.
- **Too many goals dilute focus.** Google advises three to five goals and about three key results for each.
- **The process requires regularity:** interim check-ins and a grade at the end of the period.

## Scheme

| Level | Objective | Type | Key result | Start | Target | Current | Owner | Actions | Progress |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| company | … | aspirational | … | … | … | … | … | … | 0–1.0 |
| team | … | committed | … | … | … | … | … | … | 0–1.0 |

Progress is the share of the way from "Start" to "Target". The objective's grade is roughly the average of its key results.

## How to apply

1. **Formulate three to five goals** for the period, starting at company level. Ask: what has to become true for the goal to be achieved?
2. **For each objective, set about three key results:** a number with a starting value and a target value, or a milestone like "launch X".
3. **Mark the type.** A committed objective must be met at 1.0; a shortfall requires an explanation. An aspirational one is stretched; Google's sweet spot is an average of 0.6 to 0.7.
4. **Align top-down and bottom-up:** teams propose their OKRs under the company's goals, and managers check the link.
5. **Check in mid-period** and update the current values.
6. **Grade at the end of the period** each key result from 0 to 1.0, then the objective. If aspirational OKRs consistently score 1.0, the goals are too low; if they are noticeably below 0.6, the company may be achieving less than it could. Use low grades as data for the next OKRs.

## Example

Nubank is a Brazilian digital bank. An interview with its Chief Product Officer Jag Duggal was published on the What Matters site on 24 February 2025.

The company's goal, in Duggal's words: "we want to liberate 100 million customers from financial complexity and for them to love us fanatically." Answering the question "what has to be true", the company decided to stop being only a credit card issuer, become a full-cycle bank, and go beyond Brazil.

Decision: in 2020 Nubank switched from quarterly to half-yearly OKRs. When the pandemic began, the company spent a month figuring out the situation and rewrote the goals, making them more ambitious. The main decision was expansion to Mexico, although adding a million customers in Brazil was easier than adding 10 thousand in Mexico. The OKRs were rewritten so that they were not tied to Brazil and teams valued progress in Mexico. The key results themselves are not given in the source.

Result: at the end of 2021 there were 1.4 million customers in Mexico out of 53.9 million Nu customers; in 2025 — 14 million.

Case sources: What Matters, ["Shifting Strategic Priorities with the Help of OKRs"](https://www.whatmatters.com/articles/okr-prioritization); Nu Holdings, 20-F annual reports [for 2021](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1691493/000129281422001705/nuform20f_2021.htm) and [for 2025](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1691493/000129281426002166/nuform20f_2025.htm).

## Common mistakes

- **Tasks instead of results.** "Launch a mailing" instead of "share of repeat purchases from 20% to 30%".
- **OKRs as employee assessment.** People stop setting ambitious goals to avoid a low grade.
- **All goals committed or all aspirational.** The grades cannot be read.
- **Set and forgotten.** There are no interim check-ins, and the end-of-period grade is a surprise.
- **Too many goals.** There is no priority if everything is a priority.

## Source

The method was devised by Andy Grove at Intel and described in his book "High Output Management", Random House, 1983. John Doerr brought OKRs to Google and described them in the book "Measure What Matters", Portfolio, 2018. Google's rules: re:Work, ["Set goals with OKRs"](https://web.archive.org/web/20250805130938/https://rework.withgoogle.com/en/guides/set-goals-with-okrs) and [Google OKR Playbook](https://www.whatmatters.com/resources/google-okr-playbook).
