# Balanced Scorecard

## Essence

The Balanced Scorecard is a short set of measures that gives managers a fast but comprehensive view of the business: the authors compare it to the dials and indicators in an airplane cockpit. Kaplan and Norton drew it from a year-long research project with 12 companies, on the premise "what you measure is what you get": financial measures alone, such as return on investment, can send misleading signals for continuous improvement and innovation. The scorecard adds three non-financial views next to the financial one. Each view has its own goals and measures; this card adds targets:

- **Financial:** how do we look to shareholders?
- **Customer:** how do customers see us?
- **Internal business process:** what must we excel at?
- **Innovation and learning:** can we continue to improve and create value?

## When to use and when not to

Use it when a unit has a strategy but steers only by financial results, when results arrive too late to correct anything, or when teams optimize their own number at the expense of others. Required inputs: a strategy or vision in a sentence, and someone who owns each perspective.

Do not use it as a list of everything measurable or as a bonus formula. Limits:

- **The four perspectives are a default, not a law.** Critics say the four perspectives were never formally validated; a public or non-profit unit may need other headings, with the customer or mission on top.
- **The scorecard has no overall score.** Managers read the measures together; a table of green cells proves nothing about strategy.
- **It does not choose priorities.** If the strategy is unclear, the scorecard only records the disagreement. For one period's few priorities, use a tool that ranks them.
- **Good measures do not guarantee a strategy.** The authors write that even an excellent set "does not guarantee a winning strategy"; in their example, better operations did not turn into profit.
- **The 1992 article lists goals and measures per perspective, with no numeric targets.** Targets appear only as improvement rates; this card adds the Targets column.

## Scheme

| Perspective | Question | Goals | Measures | Targets |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Financial | How do we look to shareholders? | … | … | … |
| Customer | How do customers see us? | … | … | … |
| Internal business process | What must we excel at? | … | … | … |
| Innovation and learning | Can we continue to improve and create value? | … | … | … |

Above the table sits the strategy or vision; below it, the decision the scorecard led to.

## How to apply

1. **Write the strategy** in one or two sentences. Without it the measures have nothing to be balanced against.
2. **Start with the customer.** Ask what customers expect from you, and turn each answer into a goal (for example delivery time or quality as the customer sees it).
3. **Go inside.** Name the processes and skills that decide whether the customer goals are met. These are the internal-process goals.
4. **Add innovation and learning:** what must improve for the unit to keep creating value, such as new products or staff skills.
5. **Close with finance:** what the shareholder or the budget owner needs to see if the other three work.
6. **Give each goal one or two measures,** a start value and a target with a date. Limit the number of measures to the handful that are most critical: the article presents this as the guard against information overload.
7. **Check balance:** does any measure improve at the expense of another? If yes, add the measure that exposes the trade-off.
8. **Review on a fixed rhythm.** Ask whether you are measuring the right things; replace measures that did not predict results. Write the decision: which goal, process or measure changes.

Read the result across the table: a good financial result with falling customer or process measures is a warning, and a customer gain without a process cause points to luck or a one-off.

## Example

Analog Devices, a US semiconductor company, built the first scorecard in 1987 during its 1988-1992 strategic plan, where Arthur Schneiderman owned non-financial measurement. Kaplan and Norton's 1992 article names Analog only as a company whose managers must improve delivery, cycle time, defect rate and yield at set rates. Its worked scorecard is for a disguised semiconductor firm, "Electronic Circuits Inc."; Schneiderman's claim that the article's unnamed electronics company is Analog is not in the article. Its "QIP goals" slides (QIP: Quality Improvement Process) show the scorecard in table form for integrated-circuit (IC) operations, read here from the slide of 12 November 1990:

| Perspective | Goal | Measures: 1987 / 1990 / 1992 goal |
|---|---|---|
| Financial | Market leadership, revenue growth, profitability | none on the slides read |
| Customer | Rated number 1 by customers in total value delivered | On-time delivery 85% / 96% / >99.8%; outgoing defects 500 / 230 / <10 parts per million (PPM); lead time 10 / 5.4 / <3 weeks |
| Internal | Eliminate all forms of waste | Manufacturing cycle time 15 / 8 / 4-5 weeks; process defects 5000 / 1100 / <10 PPM; yield 20% / 38% / >50% |
| Innovation and learning | Shorter time to market; reduced turnover | Time to market 36 months / N/A / 6 months; turnover target N/A |

Decisions recorded by Schneiderman: in FY1989 process measures were tracked separately for ICs and assembled products. In FY1990 delivery was measured against the customer's requested date, not the promised date; the old new-product measures, poor predictors, were replaced by a product tracking system and two measures: bookings of products introduced after 1985, and forecast third-year bookings of the current product vintage. FY1991-92 stayed unchanged. Outcomes: HP ranked Analog first among its IC suppliers in 1989 (eighth among linear IC suppliers in 1986); Dataquest named it supplier of the year in 1990 and 1991; Schneiderman does not credit the scorecard alone.

What the case lacks: financial measures and targets are not on the slides read; the 1992 cycle-time goal differs between slides (under 3 weeks in 1986, 4-5 weeks in 1990); the decisions are changes to measures, not to the business.

## Common mistakes

- **Financial measures only, relabeled.** Four headings that all hold revenue ratios restore the problem the scorecard was made to fix.
- **Too many measures.** Limiting the number of measures is the point; dozens of lines hide the few that matter.
- **Targets without a link to strategy.** Measures copied from other companies measure their strategy.
- **Measures that never change.** Analog reviewed its measures every year and replaced those that predicted poorly; a frozen scorecard drifts from the strategy.
- **Measuring on your own terms.** The article's example company counted on-time delivery against a 7-day window, while customers meant 5 or 9 days. Analog moved to the customer's requested date.
- **Using it as a pay formula at once.** Measures still being defined become targets people defend instead of questions they ask.
- **Mixing units that differ.** Analog split process measures between businesses; one combined number can hide both.
