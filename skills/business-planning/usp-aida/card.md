# USP AIDA(s) Formula

## Essence

A USP (unique selling proposition) is a short text in which the customer sees what they will get and why they should choose exactly you. AIDA(s) sets the order of the phrases of such a text: Attention, Interest, Desire, Action and satisfaction (lowercase "s"). An offer is a proposal with terms: price, discount, deadline. Here AIDA is the structure of an offer text, not a model of customer behavior.

## When to use and when not to

Apply it to an ad, an email, the first screen of a page or a product card, where there is room for several sentences. Inputs: the audience and its problem, the mechanism of the benefit, a verifiable number, the terms of the offer, a length limit.

Do not use it for a single line (a slogan): five steps do not fit into one.

Limits:

- **The PAF description (Product Architecture Framework) is short.** It contains only a table with an example, without definitions of the steps. The definitions are our reading.
- **The meaning of "s" differs.** In PAF's example it is the closing phrase about the state the buyer will reach. According to Wikipedia, Arthur Sheldon added satisfaction in 1911 to encourage repeat purchases; according to Moore's note as retold by Popik, around 1907.

## Scheme

| Step | Question to the text | Example from PAF |
|---|---|---|
| Attention | Does the customer recognize their situation? | Does the car refuse to start in the Epiphany frosts? |
| Interest | Is it said that a solution exists? | We know how to fix it! |
| Desire | What will the customer get? | A blanket for your car made from the softest Siberian valenki (felt boots), only here! |
| Action | What one step, and on what terms? | Buy in our online store with a 5% discount. |
| Satisfaction | Who will the customer become? | And become the most stylish and sharp-looking in the village! |

## How to apply

1. **Write down** the audience, the problem, the channel and the length.
2. **Attention:** the first sentence names the customer's situation in their own words.
3. **Interest:** one sentence saying that a solution exists and how it works.
4. **Desire:** the benefit and a confirmed number or argument. No data — do not write a number.
5. **Action:** one step, where it is taken, on what terms (price, discount, deadline, if they are real).
6. **Satisfaction:** the outcome for the customer. Promise only what can be delivered: a return, a guarantee, support.
7. **Read it through as a whole, through the customer's eyes,** and cut. An empty step is a decision: add it or omit it. The structure does not prove that the text will sell.
8. **Decide:** publish, rewrite or collect data. Set the check (response, returns, repeat purchases) in advance.

## Example

Dollar Shave Club (USA, a razor blade subscription, e-commerce), launched via a video on March 6, 2012; March 2012 prices are per WTOP. The AIDA(s) breakdown is ours: the company does not mention the formula, and the full text of the video is not in the verified sources.

| Step | What the sources confirm |
|---|---|
| Attention | not in the source |
| Interest | prices are compared with $20 a month for razors of well-known brands |
| Desire | simple price, home delivery |
| Action | subscription: Humble Twin — $1 a month plus delivery (5 cartridges), Executive — $9 plus delivery (3 cartridges) |
| Satisfaction | not in the source |

Decision and result: the offer was launched via a video; within 48 hours about 12,000 people subscribed, and the blades went on backorder (shipment postponed) until May. Not in the case: text by step, metrics by step, a check of "s". This is a video, not a written offer.

Sources: [WTOP, 29.03.2012](https://wtop.com/news/2012/03/razor-blade-company-brings-on-the-laughs-video/); [NBC News, 16.10.2012](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/amp/wbna49428347); launch date — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dollar_Shave_Club).

## Common mistakes

- **No attention:** the text starts with the company name.
- **Interest without a mechanism:** "We know how to fix it" without an explanation sounds like everyone else.
- **Desire without grounds:** a number without data.
- **Two actions at once** ("buy, subscribe, call"): the customer does none of them.
- **Satisfaction that cannot be delivered:** a promise without a return or support will come back as complaints.
- **All five steps in a short text:** it does not read.

## Source

Description in PAF ([Product Architecture Framework](https://productframework.ru/activities/competitive_positioning_design), Sergey Tikhomirov, productframework.ru, CC BY-SA 4.0, undated): a table with an example of five steps; the author of the formula and the definitions of the steps are not named.

Edward K. Strong ("The Psychology of Selling and Advertising", 1925) attributed the 1898 slogan "attract attention, hold interest, create desire" to E. St. Elmo Lewis; retelling: [Barry Popik](https://barrypopik.com/blog/attention_interest_desire_action_aida_of_marketing), based on Ian Moore, 2005. Akinori Iwamoto ("Marketing History Research", 2024) considers the version about Lewis weakly grounded and proposes Sheldon: [J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jmhr/3/2/3_150/_article/-char/ja). According to Wikipedia, the three-step formula was published anonymously in Printers' Ink on 9.02.1898, and Sheldon in "Successful Selling" (1911) added satisfaction (Popik, after Moore: around 1907): [AIDA (marketing)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AIDA_(marketing)). Wikipedia lists AIDAS as a variant, citing Barry and Howard, 1990.

Caveat: we did not open Strong's and Sheldon's books or the Barry and Howard article; the dates are from retellings.
