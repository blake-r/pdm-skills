# Lean Canvas

## Essence

Lean Canvas is a one-page template on which a founder records the business model of a new idea as a set of assumptions. It has nine blocks:

| Block | Question |
|---|---|
| Customer segments | for whom; who the early customers are, those whose problem is most acute |
| Problem | one to three main customer problems and how the customer solves them now |
| Unique value proposition | one phrase: how we differ and why to buy; the concept in two words |
| Solution | one solution per problem |
| Channels | the path to customers |
| Revenue streams | what customers pay for and how much |
| Cost structure | what the launch and operation will cost |
| Key metrics | a few numbers that show whether the model works |
| Unfair advantage | what cannot be quickly copied or bought |

The author of the canvas, Ash Maurya, took Alexander Osterwalder's template and replaced the four blocks describing an operating company (partners, resources, activities, customer relationships) with problem, solution, key metrics and unfair advantage.

## When to use and when not to

Use it when the idea is new and almost everything in it is an assumption: to write the model down in 20 minutes and decide which assumption to test first; when you need to compare variants of an idea or explain it to a team and investors on one page.

Do not use it for an operating business with partners and established processes: there are no blocks for partners and resources here. It does not replace a business plan or a financial model: the canvas does not calculate revenue or payback.

Limits:

- **The canvas is a snapshot, not a plan.** It is rebuilt after every significant conversation with customers.
- **The solution is deliberately small.** A narrow block is a reminder: customers care about their problem, not about your solution.
- **At the start there is usually no unfair advantage.** An empty block is normal; an invented advantage is not.

## Scheme

```
┌────────────┬────────────┬──────────────┬────────────┬────────────┐
│ Problem    │ Solution   │ Unique       │ Unfair     │ Customer   │
│            │            │ value        │ advantage  │ segments   │
│            ├────────────┤              ├────────────┤            │
│ How they   │ Key        │ Concept      │ Channels   │ Early      │
│ solve it   │ metrics    │              │            │ adopters   │
├────────────┴────────────┴──────┬───────┴────────────┴────────────┤
│ Cost structure                 │ Revenue streams                 │
└────────────────────────────────┴─────────────────────────────────┘
```

## How to apply

1. **Fill it in quickly,** no longer than 20 minutes. In a team, each person first draws their own canvas, then they merge them into one. Leave an unknown block empty.
2. **Start with segments and the problem:** who the customer is, who the early customers are, what the one to three main problems are for them and how they solve them now.
3. **Formulate the unique value proposition** from the main problem, then the solution, channels, revenue streams, costs, key metrics and, last, the unfair advantage.
4. **Mark the riskiest assumption** — the one whose failure breaks the rest of the model.
5. **Test it with customers** and rewrite the canvas based on the result: new date, new version.

## Example

TodayLaunch is a social media tool for small businesses; it is neither e-commerce nor fintech, and no public e-commerce or fintech case with a filled Lean Canvas and a decision was found. Co-founder Garrett Moon described the product's history in a guest post on Maurya's blog in 2012. The company did not publish a canvas: the blocks below were laid out by us from the text of the post.

| Block | TodayLaunch according to the post text |
|---|---|
| Segments / early adopters | small business owners; the first are those who deliberately do not choose the best-known tool |
| Problem / how it is solved now | running social media for a business is hard, the tools are confusing; HootSuite and similar services |
| Unique value proposition | one inbox for social media, as simple as email |
| Channels | meetings with local businesses, live training courses |
| Revenue streams | paid access |
| Key metrics | how many users work with the product as intended; the share of those who would be upset if the service shut down |
| Solution, costs, advantage | not formulated as separate assumptions in the post |

Source data: the first paid version produced several hundred registrations and only a few sales; then a year went into rewriting.

Decision: in spring 2012 the team killed the first paid model — "change our focus from revenue to learning" — and within a couple of weeks released a new version with half the features. A survey showed that almost 90% of users would be upset if the service shut down, and 100% of them would switch to HootSuite in that case. The team stopped pretending it did not compete with HootSuite and focused on a few key features.

Result: small releases once a month, paid access after six months; on the first day of the new version there were more registrations than in the whole previous month, and the first sale came almost immediately. No absolute numbers are given.

Case source: Garrett Moon, [guest post about TodayLaunch](https://web.archive.org/web/2020/https://blog.leanstack.com/case-study-how-a-scrappy-bootstrapped-team-in-the-middle-of-nowhere-used-lean-startup-to-achieve/), LEANSTACK blog, 27 November 2012, archived copy.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting with the solution.** The canvas describes the product, while the problem and customers are invented to fit it.
- **A "everyone" segment.** Without early customers there is no one to test assumptions on.
- **An invented unfair advantage.** "Team", "passion" or features are not advantages.
- **The canvas made once.** After conversations with customers the model has changed, but the canvas has not.
- **Testing everything at once.** The riskiest assumption has not been chosen.
