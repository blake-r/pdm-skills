# Business Model Patterns

## Essence

A business model pattern is a recurring technique on which many companies have already built their models. Researchers at the University of St. Gallen described 55 such patterns and suggest applying them to one's own model in the St. Gallen Business Model Navigator method. Its steps Initiation (describing the model), Ideation (trying on patterns) and Integration (assembling the idea) correspond to steps 1–2, 3–4 and 5–6 below. An example pattern is Razor and Blade: the base product is cheap, and the money comes from consumables.

## When to use and when not to

Use it when the current model has hit a ceiling and new ways of earning money are needed. You need a description of the current model along four questions — Who (who the customer is), What (what we offer), How (how we create it), Value (how we earn) — and a diverse team.

Do not use it as a ready-made recipe.

- **A pattern gives an idea, not a calculation.** The authors advise not to recalculate the economics at an early stage (it is usually wrong) and to test the idea with a pilot with real customers.
- **Try distant patterns too, not only near ones.** The authors advise taking techniques from other industries.
- **The catalog does not say which pattern suits you.** The choice remains with the team.

## Scheme

```
Pattern (card): name · logic · example companies
            │
Dimension │ Now               │ With the pattern
Who       │ the customer      │ …
What      │ what we offer     │ …
How       │ how we create it  │ …
Value     │ how we earn       │ …
            │
Decision · Pilot check · Risks
```

## How to apply

1. **Describe the current model** by Who–What–How–Value and name what is holding it back: a growth ceiling, price pressure, a threat.
2. **Assemble a team of 3–5 people** from different functions; it is good to have someone from outside the industry. Ban the phrase "we have always done it this way."
3. **Choose patterns.** The catalog of 55 cards: name, general logic, example company. Take near and distant ones (how many is our addition).
4. **Compare:** "how would our model change if this pattern were applied here?". For each pattern, fill in the table: what changes in Who, What, How, Value.
5. **Check consistency** with the company's competencies, the position of competitors and the value for the customer.
6. **Choose one idea and test it with a pilot** with real customers (authors). Set the success number and the deadline in advance (our addition).

## Example

**Retrospective comparison (not a case of applying the method).** Amazon, the Prime program, February 2005. Pattern No. 15 Flat Rate ("single fee"): a fixed fee regardless of usage; simpler for the customer, a steady revenue stream for the company. Amazon is not among the examples in the article, so the assignment is ours.

| | Before | With the pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Who | Amazon.com buyers | not changed (Amazon.com buyers); the benefits can be shared with four household members |
| What | delivery is paid, free with promotions | unlimited free two-day delivery on more than a million items; next day for $3.99 |
| How | not described in the sources | not described in the sources; rising delivery costs are a consequence, see Value |
| Value | Q1 2004: delivery revenue $94 million, expenses $137 million, loss $43 million | fee $79 per year; Q1 2005, Prime was in effect since February: $108 million, $163 million, loss $55 million |

Decision: launch Prime at $79 per year. The sources do not name success metrics or timing; the pilot is not mentioned in the sources. Ten years later (Fortune, 2015): according to an RBC Capital Markets survey, Prime members themselves named spending of $538 per year versus $320 for the others (self-assessment); a Forrester analyst estimated Amazon's losses on Prime delivery at no less than $1 billion per year.

What is missing: evidence that Amazon used the Navigator; "before" data for How; Prime's share of the delivery losses (the press release names free delivery and Prime together as the cause).

Sources: [Amazon press release, 26.04.2005](https://press.aboutamazon.com/2005/4/amazon-com-announces-first-quarter-financial-results-for-the-first-time-sales-of-electronics-other-general-merchandise-surpass-1-8-billion-and-comprise-25-of-total-sales-over-the-past-year); [Fortune, "Inside Amazon Prime", 03.02.2015](https://fortune.com/2015/02/03/inside-amazon-prime).

## Common mistakes

- **The load of "unlimited" is not counted.** Our conclusion, not from the source: with a single fee, heavy users cost more than light ones. Amazon's net loss on delivery in Q1 2005 grew 29% over Q1 2004, while delivery revenue grew 15%.
- **Only the price is changed.** It is not described what the pattern changes in What; changing one price does not create a new model (our conclusion, not from the source).
- **The business case is calculated before the pilot.** Early calculations are usually wrong (the authors).
- **Others' success is taken as a guarantee.** Consistency with your competencies and with the value for the customer is needed.
- **An analyst's estimate is taken as a fact.** $1 billion is a Forrester estimate; the RBC survey is a self-assessment.
